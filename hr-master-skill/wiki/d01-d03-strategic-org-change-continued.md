# HR Wiki — Domain 1-3 Continuation (Concepts 11-50)

## 11. Vertical & Horizontal Alignment of HR
**List:** A
**Definition:** Vertical alignment links HR strategy to business strategy; horizontal alignment ensures HR practices reinforce each other coherently (a "bundle").
**Application:** Vertical alignment uses the strategy cascade: business goals → workforce capabilities → HR initiatives. Horizontal alignment requires that recruitment, reward, performance, and L&D send the same signal to employees — e.g., if innovation is a strategic priority, hiring criteria, performance metrics, and reward all reinforce risk-taking.
**Expert insight:** MacDuffie (1995) showed that "bundles" of consistent HR practices in auto plants produced significantly better productivity than individual best practices in isolation. The Ulrich Centre for HR Effectiveness consistently finds horizontal misalignment is the #1 reason HR transformations fail.
**Takeaways:**
- Strategy without bundle coherence creates mixed signals to employees.
- Audit HR practices against each other, not just against industry benchmarks.
- A "strategy-on-a-page" connecting business goals → capability gaps → HR interventions is essential.

## 12. Best Fit vs Best Practice Debate
**List:** A
**Definition:** Best Fit argues HR practices must match the specific context (strategy, sector, culture); Best Practice argues universally validated practices (e.g., HPWS) deliver superior outcomes regardless of context.
**Application:** Best Fit uses Schuler & Jackson's strategy-HR matrix (innovation strategy → flexible reward; cost-reduction → tight job descriptions). Best Practice draws on Pfeffer's "Seven Practices" (employment security, selective hiring, self-managed teams, high pay, training, reduced status, info sharing).
**Expert insight:** Boxall & Purcell (2016) reconcile the debate: certain practices (employment security, voice, fair pay) are "near-universal," but their *implementation* must fit context. Pfeffer's research showed HPWS adopters outperformed peers by 30-40% in long-term ROI.
**Takeaways:**
- Treat the debate as "both/and," not "either/or."
- Universal practices need contextual implementation.
- Avoid wholesale importing of Silicon Valley practices into traditional industries without adaptation.

## 13. Resource-Based View (RBV) Applied to HR
**List:** A
**Definition:** RBV (Barney, 1991) argues sustainable competitive advantage comes from resources that are Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, and Non-substitutable (VRIN). Human capital uniquely fits all four criteria.
**Application:** Use VRIN analysis to identify "strategic" roles where human capital uniquely drives value (Boudreau & Ramstad's "pivotal" roles concept). Invest disproportionately in these roles via differential compensation, development, and retention strategies.
**Expert insight:** Wright, Dunford & Snell (2001) demonstrated that human capital, combined with social capital and organisational capital, forms a tri-component basis of HR-derived advantage. McKinsey research shows 5% of roles drive 95% of strategic value.
**Takeaways:**
- Not all roles are strategically equal — invest differentially.
- Imitability of culture and tacit knowledge is HR's strongest moat.
- RBV gives HR the language to argue for investment in board terms.

## 14. High-Performance Work Systems (HPWS)
**List:** A
**Definition:** HPWS is a coherent bundle of HR practices that increase employee ability (selective hiring, training), motivation (P4P, recognition), and opportunity (autonomy, voice) — the AMO model (Appelbaum et al., 2000).
**Application:** Typical HPWS practices: rigorous selection, extensive training, employment security, self-directed teams, high contingent pay, internal promotion, info sharing, voice mechanisms. Implementation requires top management commitment and line manager capability.
**Expert insight:** Combs, Liu, Hall & Ketchen's (2006) meta-analysis of 92 studies found HPWS adoption correlates with a 4.6% productivity increase and reduced turnover. CIPD's 2016 Productivity Through People study found HPWS users had 16% higher value-added per employee.
**Takeaways:**
- AMO is the underlying theory: ability + motivation + opportunity.
- Half-implementation underperforms doing nothing — coherence matters.
- Line manager capability is the binding constraint.

## 15. Business Acumen / Financial Literacy for HR
**List:** A
**Definition:** The ability of HR professionals to read financial statements, understand business models, calculate ROI, and engage commercially with the rest of the executive team.
**Application:** Core skills: P&L interpretation (revenue, gross margin, operating income); balance sheet basics (assets, liabilities, equity); cash flow analysis; unit economics (CAC, LTV, churn); industry-specific metrics (RevPAR for hotels, same-store sales for retail).
**Expert insight:** Ulrich's HR Competency Study (HRCS Round 8, 2021) consistently shows "Strategic Positioner" and "Credible Activist" as the two highest-impact competencies — both built on commercial fluency. SHRM's CHRO survey: 73% of CEOs say HR's commercial credibility is the #1 gating factor for elevating HR to true strategic partner.
**Takeaways:**
- HR professionals should be able to discuss EBITDA, working capital, and gross margin fluently.
- Read your CFO's investor presentations and board packs.
- Frame HR investments as cash flow / margin / risk levers.

## 16. Strategic Cost-Benefit Analysis & HR Business Case
**List:** A
**Definition:** A structured argument linking proposed HR investment to financial, operational, and strategic outcomes, with quantified costs, benefits, risks, and timeline.
**Application:** Standard structure: (1) problem/opportunity, (2) proposed solution, (3) cost (one-time + recurring), (4) benefits (quantified — productivity gain, cost avoidance, risk mitigation, revenue uplift), (5) NPV / payback period, (6) risks & dependencies, (7) recommendation. Use sensitivity analysis (best/expected/worst-case scenarios).
**Expert insight:** Boudreau & Ramstad's *Beyond HR* (2007) introduced "LAMP" (Logic-Analytics-Measures-Process) as a discipline for HR business cases. Mercer research: HR business cases with explicit NPV and risk-adjusted return are 3x more likely to win board approval.
**Takeaways:**
- Always quantify — even ranges beat qualitative claims.
- Cost avoidance often dwarfs revenue uplift in HR cases.
- Articulate the cost of inaction, not just the cost of action.

## 17. HR's Role in M&A and Restructuring
**List:** A
**Definition:** HR's involvement across the M&A lifecycle: due diligence (people/culture/comp/legal), integration planning, change management, retention of key talent, and post-merger culture realisation.
**Application:** Due diligence checklist: leadership team assessment, employee liabilities (pensions, severance, claims), compensation/benefits gaps, union situation, talent risk, cultural compatibility. Integration: Day 1 readiness, communication cascade, retention bonuses for key staff, harmonising T&Cs (legal — TUPE in UK), culture integration plan.
**Expert insight:** McKinsey's research shows 70%+ of M&A fail to deliver value, with culture and talent integration being the #1 cited cause. Deloitte's 2023 M&A study found acquirers who involved HR pre-LOI achieved 1.6x better deal synergy than those who engaged HR post-close.
**Takeaways:**
- Get HR in pre-LOI, not post-close.
- Retention of top 10% talent for 18-24 months determines value capture.
- Cultural due diligence is as important as financial due diligence.

## 18. HR Influence at Board Level
**List:** A
**Definition:** The CHRO's ability to shape strategic decisions at the board and remuneration committee level — covering CEO succession, executive pay governance, workforce reporting, and culture risk.
**Application:** Core board-level deliverables: CEO succession plan; executive remuneration recommendations to RemCo; human capital metrics in board pack; culture risk disclosure; ESG-S workforce reporting; whistleblower programme oversight. Stakeholder map: Chair, NEDs, RemCo Chair, Audit Chair, ESG Committee.
**Expert insight:** Conference Board's 2023 CHRO Effectiveness Survey: CHROs spend on average 18% of time directly with the board (vs 5% a decade ago). David Ulrich: "The CHRO is the second-most important executive after the CEO — but only if they earn it through commercial and strategic credibility."
**Takeaways:**
- Treat board members as customers — understand their concerns and language.
- Quarterly board pack human-capital section should be quant-led with narrative.
- Executive succession planning is HR's most board-relevant deliverable.

## 19. HR Governance & Policy Frameworks
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** The system of policies, procedures, decision rights, and oversight mechanisms governing how HR practices are designed, deployed, and monitored across the organisation.
**Application:** Components: policy taxonomy (mandatory/discretionary/local); policy lifecycle (draft → consult → approve → publish → train → review); ownership matrix (RACI); audit & assurance cycle; exception management; regulatory horizon scanning. Tools: policy management platforms (PolicyTech, MetricStream); HR audit frameworks (SHRM HR Audit Tool).
**Case Study:** A multinational retailer with 80 country operations discovered widely inconsistent grievance handling — some markets used 3-stage processes, others none — exposing the company to discrimination claims and brand risk. The new CHRO commissioned a global HR policy framework with three tiers: (1) Global standards (non-negotiable, e.g., anti-harassment), (2) Regional adaptations (within boundaries), (3) Local procedures (full discretion). Policy ownership was clarified (HR Ops owned the framework, country HR owned local procedures). Within 18 months, employment claims dropped 34% and audit findings reduced 60%.
**Expert insight:** SHRM HR Audit research finds policy gaps (no policy + uncodified practice) account for 67% of preventable employment claims. CIPD's *People Profession 2030* report: policy governance is the most-cited area of "future capability gap" for HR functions.
**Takeaways:**
- Three-tier policy structure (global/regional/local) balances consistency with localisation.
- Annual policy refresh cycle prevents drift.
- Audit findings should be quantified and trended like financial audits.

## 20. Strategic Workforce Planning at Enterprise Scale
**List:** A
**Definition:** A multi-year, scenario-based process for forecasting workforce demand vs supply, identifying gaps, and planning interventions (build/buy/borrow/bot) to meet future business needs.
**Application:** Steps: (1) Strategic alignment — what workforce does the business strategy require?; (2) Demand forecasting — headcount, skills, locations, capabilities over 3-5 years; (3) Supply analysis — current workforce + projected attrition, retirement, internal moves; (4) Gap analysis — surplus/shortage by role, skill, location; (5) Action plans — hiring, reskilling, redeployment, outsourcing, automation. Tools: Workday Adaptive Planning, Visier, Anaplan.
**Expert insight:** Bersin's research finds only 17% of organisations have mature SWP capability, yet those who do report 32% better workforce agility metrics. Gartner identifies skills-based SWP as the #1 emerging HR capability for 2025-2027.
**Takeaways:**
- SWP is a planning discipline, not an HRIS exercise.
- Scenario planning beats point forecasts in volatile environments.
- "Build/buy/borrow/bot" framework includes automation alongside hiring and outsourcing.

## 21. Organisational Structures (Hierarchical, Flat, Matrix, Network, Divisional, Holacracy)
**List:** A
**Definition:** Patterns of authority, reporting, grouping of work, and coordination across an organisation — directly shaping decision-making speed, cost, accountability, and culture.
**Application:** Trade-offs: Hierarchical = clear accountability but slow; Flat = fast but ambiguous accountability; Matrix = leverages expertise but dual-boss tension; Divisional = customer-focused but duplicative; Network = scalable but coordination-heavy; Holacracy = empowering but cognitively expensive. Choose based on strategy: cost leadership → functional; innovation → matrix/networked.
**Expert insight:** Galbraith's STAR model (Strategy → Structure → Processes → Rewards → People) remains the dominant design framework. Aghina, De Smet & Weerda (McKinsey 2018): "Agile organisations" combine stable backbone with dynamic networks — outperforming peers 5x.
**Takeaways:**
- Structure follows strategy, not the reverse.
- No structure is "best" — only "best fit."
- Matrix structures fail when role clarity is weak.

## 22. Organisational Culture — Definition & Formation
**List:** A
**Definition:** "The way we do things around here" — the shared assumptions, values, and behaviours that emerge from a group's response to challenges (Schein).
**Application:** Culture is shaped by: founder behaviour, what gets rewarded/punished, how leaders react to crisis, role modelling, recruitment criteria, ritual practices, and stories. Diagnosis tools: Denison Culture Survey, OCAI (Cameron & Quinn Competing Values), employee narrative analysis.
**Expert insight:** Edgar Schein argues culture operates at three levels: artefacts (visible), espoused values (declared), and underlying assumptions (taken for granted). Peter Drucker: "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." McKinsey's Culture for Performance studies: top-quartile culture organisations show 3x TSR over a decade.
**Takeaways:**
- Culture is built through behaviour, not posters.
- Senior leader behaviour is the #1 culture signal.
- Measuring culture requires qualitative + quantitative methods.

## 23. Edgar Schein's Culture Model
**List:** A
**Definition:** Schein's three-layer model: Level 1 — Artefacts (visible behaviours, dress, language, rituals); Level 2 — Espoused Values (declared values, codes of conduct); Level 3 — Basic Underlying Assumptions (unconscious beliefs that drive behaviour).
**Application:** Diagnostic approach: observe artefacts (week 1) → review espoused values (week 2) → infer underlying assumptions via probing interviews (weeks 3-6). Gaps between layers reveal cultural dysfunction — e.g., "innovation" as espoused value but risk-aversion as underlying assumption.
**Expert insight:** Schein's *Organizational Culture and Leadership* (5th ed., 2017) remains the seminal text. His maxim: "The only thing of real importance that leaders do is to create and manage culture."
**Takeaways:**
- Surface behaviour rarely reveals underlying culture.
- Diagnose all three levels before any culture change intervention.
- Misalignment between layers predicts engagement issues.

## 24. Charles Handy's Culture Types
**List:** A
**Definition:** Handy's four archetypes: Power culture (centralised, founder-led); Role culture (bureaucratic, rules-based); Task culture (project-focused, expertise-led); Person culture (individuals come first, e.g., professional partnerships).
**Application:** Diagnose your organisation's dominant type to predict behaviour: Power = fast but key-person dependent; Role = stable but slow to adapt; Task = adaptive but exhausting; Person = creative but hard to lead. Most organisations are hybrids.
**Expert insight:** Handy's *Understanding Organizations* (1985) introduced these archetypes. CIPD frequently uses Handy in Level 3-5 as introductory framework. Best used as a discussion tool, not a measurement instrument.
**Takeaways:**
- Useful for conversational diagnosis, not formal measurement.
- Founders' personalities shape Power and Person cultures heavily.
- Most large organisations migrate toward Role culture absent active counter-effort.

## 25. Hofstede's Cultural Dimensions
**List:** A
**Definition:** Six dimensions of national culture (Hofstede, IBM research 1970s-2010s): Power Distance, Individualism vs Collectivism, Masculinity vs Femininity, Uncertainty Avoidance, Long-term vs Short-term Orientation, Indulgence vs Restraint.
**Application:** Used to anticipate cross-cultural friction in global HR: e.g., high Power Distance cultures (Malaysia, Mexico) accept hierarchical performance reviews poorly when run with "peer feedback" methods designed for low PD cultures (Denmark, Israel). Adapt onboarding, performance, and leadership development by country.
**Expert insight:** Hofstede's country scores (hofstede-insights.com) remain the most-cited cross-cultural framework. Criticism: based on a single company (IBM), dimensions overlap, and country averages mask within-country variance. GLOBE study extends and partially supersedes.
**Takeaways:**
- Use as one input, not the only input.
- Within-country variance often exceeds between-country.
- Update with newer GLOBE data where available.

## 26. GLOBE Study & Trompenaars
**List:** A
**Definition:** GLOBE (House et al., 1991-2014) measures 9 cultural dimensions across 62 countries, distinguishing "as is" vs "should be" cultural values. Trompenaars (1997) offers 7 dimensions (Universalism vs Particularism, Individualism vs Communitarianism, etc.).
**Application:** GLOBE's "should be" dimension is uniquely useful for predicting leadership preferences. Trompenaars' Universalism/Particularism dimension matters greatly in compliance & ethics work (e.g., is a rule absolute or context-dependent?).
**Expert insight:** GLOBE found "Charismatic/Value-based" leadership is universally preferred but expressed differently. Trompenaars argues cultural intelligence isn't "knowing rules" but "knowing how to reconcile dilemmas across cultures."
**Takeaways:**
- GLOBE > Hofstede for predicting leadership style preferences.
- Trompenaars excels at dilemma-based intercultural decisions.
- Triangulate across multiple frameworks for global HR design.

## 27. SCARF Model — David Rock
**List:** A
**Definition:** Rock's neuroscience-based model of five social triggers that drive threat/reward responses: Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness.
**Application:** Used in change management, feedback design, performance reviews, and leadership coaching. E.g., performance feedback can trigger Status threat (criticism), Certainty threat (unclear consequences), and Fairness threat (perceived bias). Designing feedback to minimise SCARF threats increases receptivity.
**Expert insight:** Rock's NeuroLeadership Institute research finds SCARF threat reduces working memory capacity by 30-50%, explaining why threatened employees underperform on creative tasks. His *Your Brain at Work* (2009) popularised the framework.
**Takeaways:**
- Threats activate the same neural circuits as physical pain.
- Each SCARF dimension can be designed for proactively.
- Change leaders should audit interventions for SCARF impact.

## 28. McGregor Theory X / Theory Y
**List:** A
**Definition:** McGregor's two contrasting management assumptions: Theory X (people dislike work, must be controlled) vs Theory Y (people seek meaningful work, exercise self-direction).
**Application:** Diagnose management style: command-and-control, surveillance, and detailed rules suggest X assumptions; trust-based, outcome-focused, autonomous teams suggest Y. Most leaders hold mixed assumptions. Modern variants: Daniel Pink's Drive (autonomy/mastery/purpose), Laloux's Teal organisations.
**Expert insight:** McGregor's *The Human Side of Enterprise* (1960) is foundational. Recent research (Wharton, MIT) confirms Y-aligned organisations show 17-23% higher discretionary effort. But contingency view: Theory X may fit some routine or high-risk environments.
**Takeaways:**
- Most management dysfunction stems from unstated X assumptions.
- Y assumptions drive engagement but require capability investment.
- Modern self-managing teams are Theory Y at organisational level.

## 29. Job Design — Hackman-Oldham Model
**List:** A
**Definition:** The Job Characteristics Model: five core job characteristics (skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback) drive three psychological states (meaningfulness, responsibility, knowledge of results) → motivation, performance, satisfaction.
**Application:** Calculate Motivating Potential Score (MPS) = [(skill variety + task identity + task significance)/3] × autonomy × feedback. Redesign jobs by increasing each dimension: job enrichment (vertical loading), job enlargement (horizontal), task combining, client relationships, feedback channels.
**Expert insight:** Hackman & Oldham (1976) developed the model from research at AT&T. Meta-analyses confirm MPS predicts 20-35% of variance in work motivation. Recent extensions: Grant's prosocial job design research (showing impact on beneficiaries boosts motivation).
**Takeaways:**
- Autonomy is the #1 lever — multiplicative, not additive.
- Feedback closure is the most neglected dimension.
- Boring jobs can be enriched without restructure via task combining.

## 30. Span of Control & Org Design Principles
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Span of control = number of direct reports per manager. Closely related: depth (number of layers), centralisation vs decentralisation, formalisation, grouping principles (function/product/geography).
**Application:** Optimal span depends on task complexity, manager capability, employee expertise, and technology. Typical ranges: 4-7 for complex interdependent work; 12-25 for routine standardised work. Wider spans flatten hierarchies, reduce cost, but stress managers. Use task analysis to set appropriate spans.
**Case Study:** A UK financial services firm conducted a "delayering" exercise after benchmarking against peers revealed 12 reporting layers and average span of 4.2. Top-down redesign reset average span to 7.8 and reduced layers to 8. Savings: £18M annual management cost. But within 18 months, two issues emerged: (1) middle managers reported burnout from doubled spans; (2) developmental coaching dropped sharply (less manager attention per report). Remediation included manager capability uplift, team leader roles (sub-supervisory), and reinvesting 25% of savings in management development.
**Expert insight:** McKinsey's Org Solutions research: optimal span depends on Manager Capability × Task Complexity × Employee Expertise. Deloitte's Human Capital Trends consistently finds delayering without manager capability investment backfires within 24 months.
**Takeaways:**
- Span is contingent on task and capability — no universal "right number."
- Delayering without manager capability investment causes burnout.
- Sub-supervisory roles (team leads) can extend effective span.

## 31. Sociotechnical Systems & Contingency Theory
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Sociotechnical Systems Theory (Trist & Bamforth, Tavistock Institute) argues optimal performance requires joint optimisation of social systems (people, relationships) and technical systems (tools, processes). Contingency theory (Lawrence & Lorsch, Fiedler) argues no single best way to organise — depends on environment and task.
**Application:** When implementing new technology (e.g., automation, AI tools), redesign both the technology *and* the social system simultaneously: roles, team boundaries, decision rights, skill mix. Common failure: deploying ERP/AI without re-engineering work patterns and team structures.
**Case Study:** A UK NHS trust implemented an electronic patient records system in 2019. The "go-live" focused entirely on technical training. Within 6 months, clinician productivity dropped 23%, error rates rose, and burnout surged. A sociotechnical re-design six months later involved: redrawing team roles around the new system, embedding ward champions, redesigning handover protocols around system data, and reskilling administrators. Productivity recovered and exceeded baseline within 18 months. Tavistock principles applied: joint design, redundant skills, autonomous work teams.
**Expert insight:** The Tavistock coal-mining studies (1950s) remain seminal: same technology + same workers + different social system = 70% productivity difference. Lawrence & Lorsch's contingency research shows differentiation/integration needs vary by environmental uncertainty.
**Takeaways:**
- Never deploy new technology without redesigning the social system.
- Joint optimisation outperforms sequential optimisation.
- No "one best way" — context determines structure.

## 32. Open Systems Thinking
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Conceptualising organisations as living systems that exchange resources, information, and energy with their environment — adapting through feedback loops, requisite variety, and ongoing equilibrium.
**Application:** Apply to HR: see HR practices not as standalone but as interacting subsystems. E.g., performance management interacts with reward, recruitment, and L&D. Change in one ripples to others. Use systems mapping (causal loop diagrams, stocks and flows) to anticipate unintended consequences.
**Case Study:** A tech company introduced aggressive stack-ranking. Open systems analysis (post-failure) revealed cascading consequences: stack-ranking → reduced peer collaboration → degraded knowledge sharing → slower onboarding → higher new-hire churn → recruiting pressure → lowered hiring bar → degraded talent → vicious cycle. Systems mapping at design time would have surfaced these loops. The company replaced stack-ranking with calibration-based development conversations.
**Expert insight:** Senge's *The Fifth Discipline* (1990) operationalised systems thinking for organisations. Peter Checkland's Soft Systems Methodology offers a practitioner toolkit. The Cynefin framework (Snowden) extends systems thinking to complex/chaotic domains.
**Takeaways:**
- HR interventions always have second-order effects.
- Map systems before redesigning components.
- Embrace requisite variety — match internal complexity to environmental complexity.

## 33. Organisational Development (OD) Discipline
**List:** A
**Definition:** A planned, systematic, evidence-based approach to improving organisational effectiveness through people-focused interventions, grounded in humanistic values, action research, and behavioural science.
**Application:** Classic OD process: entry → diagnosis → feedback → action planning → intervention → evaluation. Practitioner role is facilitator, not expert. Distinguished from change management by its values base (humanistic), methodology (action research), and developmental aim (capability building).
**Expert insight:** Kurt Lewin (1947) founded OD. Cummings & Worley's *Organisation Development and Change* is the standard reference. NTL Institute remains the leading OD practitioner training body. Modern critique: classic OD's pace doesn't match Agile/digital cadence — hence emergence of "Agile OD."
**Takeaways:**
- OD is a discipline, not a synonym for "HR projects."
- Humanistic values distinguish OD from pure change management.
- Action research = diagnose with the system, not on the system.

## 34. OD Interventions — Process Consultation, Appreciative Inquiry, Team Building
**List:** A
**Definition:** Structured methods to improve organisational functioning: Process Consultation (Schein) — observe and feed back team dynamics; Appreciative Inquiry (Cooperrider) — discover/dream/design/deliver from strengths; Team Building — Tuckman stages, role clarity, team charters.
**Application:** Choose intervention based on diagnosis: process consultation for dysfunctional team dynamics; AI for organisations stuck in deficit-thinking; team building for newly formed or recently restructured teams. Large-group interventions (Open Space, World Café) for whole-organisation engagement.
**Expert insight:** Appreciative Inquiry has been used in major transformations (US Navy, British Airways). Cooperrider's research shows AI generates ~40% more implementable ideas than deficit-based methods. Schein's PC asks: "What is going on here and how can we make it better?"
**Takeaways:**
- Match intervention type to diagnosis.
- Strengths-based methods reduce resistance vs problem-focused.
- The facilitator's stance — humble inquiry — matters more than the technique.

## 35. Holacracy / Self-Organising Teams
**List:** A
**Definition:** A non-hierarchical governance system distributing authority through "circles" of roles rather than managers. Decisions are made via structured "governance meetings" with role-based authority (Brian Robertson, HolacracyOne).
**Application:** Replace job descriptions with "roles" (cluster of accountabilities); replace managers with "lead links"; use tactical and governance meetings to evolve structure. Adopted notably by Zappos, Medium (later abandoned), Buurtzorg.
**Expert insight:** Frédéric Laloux's *Reinventing Organisations* (2014) frames Holacracy within the "Teal" stage of org evolution. Mixed evidence: Zappos retention dipped post-adoption; Buurtzorg's nurse self-management model demonstrates strong success in healthcare. Most "Holacratic" organisations adopt partial elements.
**Takeaways:**
- Pure Holacracy is rare and demanding.
- Self-organising principles can be adopted partially.
- Requires high transparency, conflict capability, and process discipline.

## 36. Psychological Safety
**List:** A
**Definition:** "A shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking" — Amy Edmondson. Psychological safety enables team members to speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes, and challenge the status quo without fear of humiliation.
**Application:** Build PS through: leader modelling fallibility ("I don't know — what do you think?"); productive responses to bad news; framing work as learning (not execution); inviting input by name; ritualising "after-action reviews"; rewarding voice over silence.
**Expert insight:** Edmondson's *The Fearless Organization* (2018) synthesises 25+ years of research. Google's Project Aristotle (2015) identified PS as the #1 predictor of high-performing teams, above skills, structure, or seniority. Edmondson: "PS is not about being nice — it's about candour."
**Takeaways:**
- PS doesn't lower standards — it enables higher standards by enabling candour.
- Leader behaviour creates PS faster than any policy.
- Measure PS with the 7-item Edmondson scale.

## 37. Human Capital Theory
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Economic theory (Becker, 1964) that views education, training, and health investments in workers as capital — generating measurable returns through higher productivity and earnings.
**Application:** Apply to HR: justify L&D investment using ROI/IRR economics. Specific vs general training distinction (Becker): general training (transferable) is paid for by workers; specific training (firm-only value) is paid for by firms — explaining why firms underinvest in general skills training. Modern critique: theory undervalues tacit knowledge and relational capital.
**Case Study:** A UK manufacturer faced 28% annual attrition in skilled CNC operators. Conventional wisdom: invest more in training. But Becker's theory predicted under-investment in general skills as workers would leave once trained. The CHRO redesigned the programme: (1) firm-specific elements (proprietary tooling) funded by the company; (2) general elements (industry certifications) co-funded by apprentices via salary deductions repayable on departure within 3 years. Attrition fell to 14% and ROI on training rose 220%.
**Expert insight:** Becker won the 1992 Nobel Prize partly for Human Capital theory. World Bank's Human Capital Index (2018) operationalised it at national level. CIPD's research on "people analytics maturity" frames human capital as financial-statement-worthy.
**Takeaways:**
- General vs firm-specific distinction matters for who pays.
- Repayment clauses solve the "trained-then-leave" problem.
- Human Capital Theory underpins modern people analytics.

## 38. Lewin's 3-Stage Change Model
**List:** A
**Definition:** Kurt Lewin's (1947) three-stage model: Unfreeze (create dissatisfaction with status quo, increase change motivation), Change/Move (implement new state), Refreeze (embed new state as default).
**Application:** Practical steps: Unfreeze — share burning platform data, leadership communication, structured stakeholder engagement. Change — pilot, training, role redesign, system changes. Refreeze — celebrate wins, update policies/comp/PM, hard-wire new behaviours into recruitment and onboarding.
**Expert insight:** Lewin remains the foundational framework — most modern models (Kotter, ADKAR) build on it. Critique: "refreeze" doesn't fit volatile environments where continuous change is the norm. Burnes (2004) argues Lewin's model is more nuanced than its caricature.
**Takeaways:**
- Don't underestimate Unfreeze — most changes fail by skipping it.
- Refreeze fails without structural reinforcement (policy, reward, PM).
- Treat the model as iterative, not linear, in volatile contexts.

## 39. Kotter's 8-Step Change Model
**List:** A
**Definition:** John Kotter's 8 steps: (1) Create urgency, (2) Form a guiding coalition, (3) Develop vision and strategy, (4) Communicate the vision, (5) Empower action, (6) Generate short-term wins, (7) Consolidate gains, (8) Anchor in culture.
**Application:** Sequential application: Steps 1-4 set the stage; 5-7 enable action and momentum; 8 sustains. Common failure: skipping urgency, weak coalition, under-communicating (Kotter says by factor of 10x), and not consolidating before declaring victory.
**Expert insight:** Kotter's *Leading Change* (1996) and HBR article are foundational. His research finds 70% of change initiatives fail — usually at steps 1-4. Updated *Accelerate!* (2014) introduces "dual operating system" — hierarchy + network for agile change.
**Takeaways:**
- Most failures occur in steps 1-4 (set-up).
- Communicate 10x what feels sufficient.
- Short-term wins (step 6) prevent change fatigue.

## 40. ADKAR / Prosci Change Model
**List:** A
**Definition:** Prosci's individual change model: Awareness (of need), Desire (to support), Knowledge (how to change), Ability (to implement), Reinforcement (to sustain).
**Application:** Diagnose individual or group readiness at each stage; design interventions to address the weakest letter. Awareness gap → communication; Desire gap → engagement & WIIFM; Knowledge gap → training; Ability gap → coaching/practice; Reinforcement gap → recognition, metrics.
**Expert insight:** Prosci's Best Practices in Change Management research (8 editions, 1,500+ studies) finds ADKAR-disciplined changes are 6x more likely to meet objectives. ADKAR's value: individual-centred — change happens one person at a time.
**Takeaways:**
- ADKAR diagnoses where individuals are stuck.
- Knowledge ≠ Ability — practice closes the gap.
- Reinforcement is the most-neglected stage.

## 41. Bridges Transition Model
**List:** A
**Definition:** William Bridges distinguished change (external, situational) from transition (internal, psychological). His three phases: Ending (letting go), Neutral Zone (in-between, ambiguous), New Beginning (new identity, energy).
**Application:** Recognise that even "good" changes require grieving what's lost. In the Neutral Zone, productivity dips and ambiguity peaks — provide stability, communication, and quick wins. New Beginning requires clear identity, capability, and purpose.
**Expert insight:** Bridges' *Managing Transitions* (1991) is the classic reference. His insight: organisations manage change events but neglect personal transitions, which is why technically successful changes fail in adoption. Especially relevant in M&A integration.
**Takeaways:**
- Change is external; transition is internal.
- The Neutral Zone is where the work happens — and where it breaks.
- Honour what's ending to enable what's beginning.

## 42. Kübler-Ross Change Curve
**List:** A
**Definition:** Adapted from her grief stages, Kübler-Ross's curve applied to change: Shock → Denial → Anger → Bargaining → Depression → Acceptance → Integration. Performance dips during the middle stages.
**Application:** Map stakeholders onto the curve; design support for each stage. Communication strategies: shock — acknowledge; denial — repeat evidence; anger — listen and validate; bargaining — engage in shaping; depression — provide stability; acceptance — celebrate. The curve is non-linear — people loop back.
**Expert insight:** Originally developed for terminal illness (1969), the curve's application to organisational change has been criticised as conceptually loose but practically useful. Best used as a sense-making tool, not a rigid framework.
**Takeaways:**
- Expect performance dip — plan for it.
- Anger and bargaining stages are valuable, not obstacles.
- People loop back — change is not monotonic.

## 43. Lewin's Force Field Analysis
**List:** A
**Definition:** Diagnostic tool: identify driving forces (pushing toward change) and restraining forces (pushing back), with relative weights. Change is achieved by strengthening drivers, weakening restrainers, or both.
**Application:** Run as a workshop: list drivers and restrainers on a T-chart; weight each (1-5); brainstorm interventions for each force. Lewin's insight: it's more effective to remove restrainers than add drivers — adding drivers raises tension; removing restrainers releases existing energy.
**Expert insight:** Lewin's original concept (1943). Used widely in OD, lean management, and HR change. Modern variant: SWOT-style 2x2 analysis with internal/external dimensions.
**Takeaways:**
- Remove obstacles before adding incentives.
- Quantitative weighting forces prioritisation.
- Best done with stakeholders, not for them.

## 44. Change Readiness Assessment
**List:** A
**Definition:** A structured diagnostic of an organisation's capacity to absorb and sustain a planned change — covering sponsorship, communication, capability, culture, and capacity dimensions.
**Application:** Tools: Prosci PCT (Project Change Triangle); Bridges Readiness Survey; custom diagnostics covering: change history (success rate), leader sponsorship strength, change saturation (concurrent changes), employee trust, resource availability, capability for new state. Score across dimensions; prioritise interventions to close gaps before launch.
**Expert insight:** Prosci research: change projects with readiness diagnostics applied at design phase are 4x more likely to meet objectives. McKinsey's transformation studies confirm change saturation is the most under-attended risk.
**Takeaways:**
- Diagnose before designing.
- Change saturation kills more changes than resistance.
- Sponsor strength is the #1 readiness factor.

## 45. Resistance to Change
**List:** A
**Definition:** Behaviours and attitudes opposing planned change, ranging from active (sabotage, vocal opposition) to passive (compliance without commitment). Sources: loss aversion, identity threat, status loss, uncertainty, fairness concerns, past betrayals, capability anxiety.
**Application:** Diagnose source first (don't assume). Strategies: provide information (if knowledge gap); engagement (if voice gap); coaching/training (if capability gap); negotiation (if loss is real); coercion (only as last resort). Kotter & Schlesinger's 6 strategies frame this comprehensively.
**Expert insight:** Resistance is data, not obstruction — it surfaces design flaws and unmet needs. Ford & Ford (2009) argue much "resistance" is actually a rational response to poor change design. CIPD: "Resistance is the system telling you what you got wrong."
**Takeaways:**
- Treat resistance as feedback, not obstacle.
- Diagnose the source — different sources need different responses.
- "Resistors" often become the strongest advocates if engaged.

## 46. Change Communication Planning
**List:** A
**Definition:** A structured plan for who needs to know what, when, how, and from whom — covering audience segmentation, key messages, channels, cadence, and feedback loops.
**Application:** Components: stakeholder map; message house (anchor → 3-5 supporting points → evidence); channel matrix (town halls, email, manager cascades, intranet, video); cadence calendar; spokesperson assignment; two-way mechanisms (Q&A, pulse surveys). Manager cascade is the single most effective channel.
**Expert insight:** Kotter's "10x rule" — communicate 10x more than you think necessary. Bersin's research: employees need to hear key messages 7+ times from 3+ sources to internalise. Edelman Trust Barometer: direct manager is the #1 trusted communicator for change.
**Takeaways:**
- Manager cascade outperforms mass comms.
- Repeat far more than feels comfortable.
- Two-way mechanisms (not just broadcast) are essential.

## 47. Change Leadership vs Change Management
**List:** A
**Definition:** Change management = the disciplined process and toolkit (plans, comms, training); Change leadership = the human capacity to inspire, hold ambiguity, model new behaviours, and personally drive transformation.
**Application:** Both required: management without leadership becomes mechanical and resisted; leadership without management is inspirational but unstructured. Build both: management via Prosci/Kotter discipline; leadership via executive coaching, vision crafting, and personal commitment.
**Expert insight:** Kotter's distinction: managers cope with complexity; leaders cope with change. CHRO's role often involves both: structuring the change while modelling the behaviour. McKinsey's transformation research: leadership shortfall is the #1 cause of transformation failure (62%).
**Takeaways:**
- Discipline + Inspiration, not either/or.
- Senior leader behaviour is the change.
- HR's role is to build both capacities in line leaders.

## 48. Sustaining Change Post-Implementation
**List:** A
**Definition:** Embedding new ways of working so they become permanent — through structural reinforcement (policies, systems, metrics) and cultural reinforcement (rituals, stories, leader behaviour).
**Application:** Sustainment tactics: update performance management criteria; revise hiring profiles; embed new behaviours in onboarding; modify reward structure; institute ongoing measurement; celebrate exemplars; periodic refresh communications. The first 12-18 months post-go-live is highest regression risk.
**Expert insight:** Prosci finds 50% of changes regress within 18 months if sustainment isn't designed. Kotter step 8 ("Anchor in culture") is the most neglected step. CIPD: "Change isn't sustained until it's invisible — until it's just how things are."
**Takeaways:**
- Design sustainment from day 1, not post-launch.
- Hardwire change into HR systems (PM, reward, hiring).
- Measure regression risk, not just initial adoption.

## 49. Agile Working & Organisational Agility
**List:** A
**Definition:** Agility = the capacity to sense and respond rapidly to changing conditions through small autonomous teams, iterative delivery, distributed decision rights, and continuous learning. Originated in software; now applied organisation-wide.
**Application:** Practices: cross-functional squads; product-based (not project-based) teams; OKRs; sprint cadence; retrospectives; minimal viable product approach; quarterly business reviews. HR implications: skills-based teams, dynamic role design, rapid talent reallocation, agile performance management.
**Expert insight:** McKinsey's *Agile Organisations* research: agile firms achieve 30% better financial performance, customer satisfaction, and employee engagement. ING's bank-wide agile transformation (2015) is a landmark case. Bain & Co finds agility maturity correlates with 2-3x revenue growth.
**Takeaways:**
- Agility is structural and cultural, not just methodological.
- HR must redesign performance, reward, and career for agile contexts.
- "Agile theatre" (rituals without substance) is the #1 failure mode.

## 50. Innovation & Creativity in Organisations
**List:** A
**Definition:** Innovation = the implementation of new products, services, processes, or business models that create value. Creativity = the generation of novel and useful ideas (Amabile). Both require psychological safety, slack time, diversity, and tolerance of failure.
**Application:** Build innovation capability via: dedicated time (Google's 20%, 3M's 15%); cross-functional collaboration; diverse teams; idea-management systems; staged funding (idea → prototype → pilot → scale); rewarding learning from failure; intrapreneurship programmes.
**Expert insight:** Amabile's research at HBS shows intrinsic motivation, expertise, and creative thinking skills as the three components. Tom Kelley (IDEO) operationalised design thinking. Innovation leaders cited (Bain): Amazon, Apple, Tesla — all share psychological safety, customer obsession, and willingness to bet.
**Takeaways:**
- Innovation requires slack — busy organisations don't innovate.
- Diverse teams produce more breakthrough ideas.
- Psychological safety is the #1 enabler.

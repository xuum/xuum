# HR Wiki — Domain 8: Total Rewards & Compensation + Domain 9: Employee & Labour Relations

## 141. Total Reward Framework — Armstrong & Brown
**List:** A
**Definition:** Michael Armstrong & Duncan Brown's framework integrating all elements of reward — pay, benefits, learning, work environment — into a coherent strategy that supports business goals and the employee value proposition.
**Application:** Four quadrants: Transactional (pay, benefits) and Relational (learning, work environment). Strategic deployment aligns the mix to business needs: knowledge-intensive firms emphasise relational; sales firms emphasise transactional incentives.
**Expert insight:** Armstrong's *Reward Management* (15th ed.) is the canonical CIPD reference. CIPD Reward Survey: 67% of firms claim "total reward strategy" but only 23% have measured employee perception of it. WorldatWork's Total Rewards Model adds Career and Performance as standalone components.
**Takeaways:**
- Reward is more than pay — employees value the relational components.
- Communicate Total Reward visibly via Total Compensation Statements.
- Strategic mix matters more than absolute level.

## 142. Internal Equity vs External Competitiveness
**List:** A
**Definition:** Internal equity = fair pay relationships between roles within the organisation. External competitiveness = pay levels relative to the market.
**Application:** Tools: job evaluation for internal equity; market pricing/surveys for external competitiveness. Trade-offs: tight internal equity restricts ability to match market pay for hot skills; aggressive external benchmarking creates internal pay inequities. Most organisations balance both via salary ranges and adjustment policies.
**Expert insight:** Mercer: 60% of organisations cite "pay-equity vs market-pay tension" as their top reward dilemma. Pay transparency laws (NY, CA, CO, EU Directive 2026) are forcing more visible internal equity.
**Takeaways:**
- Both lenses are essential — neither alone suffices.
- Hot-skill premiums create equity tensions — manage transparently.
- Pay transparency makes equity inconsistencies highly visible.

## 143. Pay Structures — Bands, Broad-Banding, Spines, Compa-Ratio
**List:** A
**Definition:** Pay Structure = the organisation's framework for grouping roles into grades with associated pay ranges. Broad-Banding = fewer, wider bands. Pay Spine = incremental scale (common in public sector). Compa-Ratio = actual pay / midpoint × 100.
**Application:** Choose structure: narrow bands for tight control and clear progression; broad bands for flexibility and skill-based growth; spines for transparency and union environments. Compa-ratio targets: 90-95% for new joiners; 100-105% for solid performers; 110-120% for top performers.
**Expert insight:** WorldatWork: broad-banding adoption peaked in 2000s but has declined as firms seek tighter control. Public sector pay spines (UK NHS, civil service) face criticism for inflexibility. Compa-ratio analysis surfaces pay equity issues.
**Takeaways:**
- Structure should reflect strategy (control vs flexibility).
- Compa-ratio is the single most useful diagnostic metric.
- Annual range refresh prevents structures becoming stale.

## 144. Job Evaluation — Analytical & Non-Analytical
**List:** A
**Definition:** Systematic process to determine relative role value internally. Analytical methods (point-factor, factor comparison) score against weighted factors. Non-analytical (ranking, paired comparison, classification) compare whole jobs.
**Application:** Major schemes: Hay Method (Korn Ferry), Mercer IPE, Willis Towers Watson Global Grading, NJC scheme (UK local government). Analytical methods provide stronger defence in equal pay claims. Implementation: develop/adopt scheme; train evaluators; evaluate; ranking review; pay structure design.
**Expert insight:** UK Equal Pay law (Equality Act 2010) requires defensible job evaluation for "work of equal value" claims. Hay/Korn Ferry's Method covers 5+ million jobs globally and remains the most widely used analytical scheme.
**Takeaways:**
- Analytical evaluation provides legal defence against pay claims.
- Scheme choice is strategic — affects pay relativities materially.
- Re-evaluation every 5-7 years prevents drift.

## 145. Market Pricing & Salary Surveys
**List:** A
**Definition:** Process of benchmarking roles against external market data — using salary surveys to determine competitive pay levels. Major surveys: Mercer, Korn Ferry, WTW, Aon, Radford (tech), industry-specific surveys.
**Application:** Methodology: match roles via job descriptions (not titles); use multiple sources; weight by relevance; aggregate to percentile (typically 50th = median, 75th = top quartile); compare to current pay; adjust ranges. Annual market analysis is standard.
**Expert insight:** WorldatWork: 78% of organisations participate in 3+ salary surveys. Self-reported salary data (Glassdoor, LinkedIn) is unreliable — formal surveys remain the gold standard. Hot-skills indexing (e.g., AI, cybersecurity) tracks faster than annual surveys.
**Takeaways:**
- Multiple sources are essential for accuracy.
- Match roles, not titles.
- Hot-skill markets require off-cycle adjustments.

## 146. Pay Positioning — Lead, Match, Lag
**List:** A
**Definition:** Strategic decision on where to position pay relative to the market: Lead (above median, e.g., 75th percentile); Match (at median); Lag (below median, often offset by other rewards).
**Application:** Choose by strategy: Lead for talent-intensive industries (consulting, banking, tech); Match for most firms; Lag for cost-led industries (retail, hospitality) often paired with stronger non-cash rewards. Differential positioning by role family is common: lead on technical, match on support.
**Expert insight:** WorldatWork: 65% of firms position at-median for base, but vary on total reward. Pay positioning visibly affects time-to-fill and quality of hire. Below-median firms must compensate elsewhere or accept higher turnover.
**Takeaways:**
- Positioning must be intentional, not accidental.
- Differential positioning by role family is sophisticated practice.
- Lagging requires strong non-cash compensating differential.

## 147. Geographic Pay Differentials
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Pay variations by location reflecting differences in cost of living, labour market wages, taxation, and competitive pressures.
**Application:** Approaches: zone-based (high/medium/low-cost markets); city-specific multipliers; hybrid-work adjusted differentials (location-based reduction for remote workers). Post-COVID complexity: how to pay employees who moved to lower-cost regions.
**Case Study:** A US tech firm faced 30% of staff relocating to lower-cost regions during 2020-2022. Initial policy: maintain pay regardless of location. Result: pay inequity vs locally-hired peers in new locations; budget concerns; resentment from those who didn't relocate. New policy (2023): location-adjusted pay using zones; new hires paid locally; existing relocators given 24-month transition with capped reductions; transparent communication of methodology. Result: budget control restored; some attrition (5%); fairness perception stable.
**Expert insight:** Mercer Global Talent Trends: 62% of multi-location firms now use formal geographic differentials. Facebook/Meta and Google's 2021 location-based pay cuts for remote workers triggered industry debate on fairness vs market logic.
**Takeaways:**
- Geographic differentials are now standard post-COVID.
- Transparent methodology beats covert adjustments.
- "Same pay everywhere" is rarely sustainable beyond a startup phase.

## 148. Pay Compression & Inversion
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Pay Compression = small pay differential between roles of different seniority (e.g., senior earning only slightly more than junior). Pay Inversion = junior earning more than senior (typically due to market-rate hires).
**Application:** Causes: market rate increases outpacing internal increments; freezes on existing staff while new hires get market rate; promotion increments not keeping pace. Resolution: spot adjustments; off-cycle reviews; structural raises; transparent communication.
**Case Study:** A UK financial services firm in 2022 faced acute pay compression in technology roles after hiring developers at +25% market premiums. Existing developers (paid below new joiners by 15-20%) discovered the gap through internal salary survey leaks. Engagement scores in tech dropped 22 points; attrition spiked. Resolution: emergency pay action across 280 staff totalling £4.1M; transparent communication on methodology; commitment to ongoing market alignment. Lesson: addressing compression early is far cheaper than addressing it after disclosure.
**Expert insight:** Korn Ferry: 41% of firms cite pay compression as a top reward issue post-2022 inflation. Mercer: median pay raises lagged inflation, exacerbating compression for tenured staff.
**Takeaways:**
- Compression is a slow-growing crisis until it explodes.
- New-hire premiums must be matched by tenured-staff adjustments.
- Transparency in pay practices is the only stable equilibrium.

## 149. Merit Pay & Pay-for-Performance
**List:** A
**Definition:** Compensation increases or bonuses linked to individual performance ratings — typically delivered via annual merit cycles with differentiated increases by performance level.
**Application:** Standard practice: merit matrix combining performance rating (e.g., 1-5) and position-in-range (compa-ratio); allocations from a defined budget pool; manager review; calibration; communication. Effective practices: meaningful differentiation (top performer increment ≥ 2x average).
**Expert insight:** Alfie Kohn's *Punished by Rewards* (1993) critiques extrinsic motivators; Daniel Pink's *Drive* (2009) finds P4P works for routine tasks but undermines creative work. WorldatWork: 87% of organisations use P4P despite mixed evidence — for cultural and budget-discipline reasons.
**Takeaways:**
- Differentiation must be meaningful or P4P loses signal.
- P4P fits routine tasks better than creative/knowledge work.
- Forced ranking has largely fallen out of favour.

## 150. Individual Performance-Related Pay (IPRP)
**List:** A
**Definition:** Pay system linking individual pay progression directly to performance assessment — typically annual review determines increment within band, bonus level, or progression to higher band.
**Application:** Design choices: how much pay is at risk; how performance is measured; calibration mechanisms; communication of pay decisions. Stakeholder-aligned IPRP works best in roles with clear individual measurable output (sales, claims processing). Less effective for interdependent team work.
**Expert insight:** Marsden & Richardson (LSE): IPRP often fails to motivate because employees doubt the fairness of ratings. CIPD: 53% of organisations using IPRP find it doesn't drive desired behaviour. Hybrid models (team + individual) increasingly popular.
**Takeaways:**
- IPRP requires high-trust performance management.
- Hybrid (individual + team) plans are increasingly used.
- Manager calibration is the make-or-break.

## 151. Variable Pay / Short-Term Incentives
**List:** A
**Definition:** Compensation contingent on achievement of specified results within a defined period (typically annual). Forms: bonuses, commission, profit-sharing, gain-sharing, spot awards.
**Application:** Design choices: metric selection (financial, customer, operational); weighting; threshold/target/stretch; payout curve; funding (self-funding vs corporate pool). Best practice: 3-5 metrics; clear line of sight; quarterly check-ins.
**Expert insight:** WorldatWork: 95% of organisations offer some variable pay; average payout 10-15% of base for individual contributors, 30-50% for executives. Behavioural research: STIs effective when line-of-sight is direct.
**Takeaways:**
- Too many metrics dilute focus (max 3-5).
- Line of sight is essential — employees must influence the outcomes.
- Self-funding plans are most defensible to finance.

## 152. Long-Term Incentives — LTIs, RSUs, Stock Options
**List:** A
**Definition:** Equity or cash compensation vesting over multiple years (typically 3-4) tied to long-term performance or service. Forms: Stock Options, RSUs (Restricted Stock Units), Performance Share Units, LTIPs, cash-based long-term plans.
**Application:** Vesting schedules: 4-year cliff or graduated (e.g., 25% per year); performance LTIs require achievement of multi-year metrics (TSR, EPS, ROCE). Target audiences: executives, senior leaders, increasingly broader rollout (e.g., Amazon, tech firms granting RSUs broadly).
**Expert insight:** Mercer: LTIs comprise 40-60% of CEO total reward in S&P 500; 25-35% for top execs. Glass Lewis & ISS govern proxy voting on LTI design. Performance LTIs (vs time-based) dominant in UK FTSE 350.
**Takeaways:**
- LTIs align with long-term shareholder interests.
- Performance LTIs require defensible metric selection.
- Tax treatment varies wildly — coordinate with finance/tax.

## 153. Executive Compensation Design & Governance
**List:** A
**Definition:** Design and governance of compensation for senior executives (typically CEO, top 5-10) — encompassing base, STI, LTI, benefits, perks, severance, retention, and clawback provisions.
**Application:** Governance: independent Remuneration Committee; external advisors; benchmarking; shareholder consultation; "binding say-on-pay" votes (UK). Key components: base (15-25% of total); STI (15-30%); LTI (40-65%); benefits/perks (5-10%).
**Expert insight:** UK Corporate Governance Code requires extensive exec pay disclosure. Mercer & Glass Lewis: executive pay ratios (CEO:median) average 250-350x in S&P 500. Pay-for-failure (severance unrelated to performance) is a major proxy-vote red flag.
**Takeaways:**
- Governance scrutiny has intensified — design for transparency.
- Pay-for-failure (golden parachutes) attracts shareholder revolt.
- Clawback provisions are increasingly standard.

## 154. CEO Pay Ratio & Say-on-Pay
**List:** A
**Definition:** CEO Pay Ratio = CEO total comp / median employee total comp, disclosed annually (US Dodd-Frank §953(b); UK CGC 2018). Say-on-Pay = shareholder advisory or binding vote on executive pay (US: advisory; UK: binding every 3 years).
**Application:** Reporting required for FTSE 350 in UK and US public companies. Ratios range from 5-10x in small firms to 2000+ in mega-corps. Significant shareholder pushback when ratio inflated by stock awards in good years.
**Expert insight:** AFL-CIO Pay Watch: US S&P 500 average CEO:worker ratio 272:1 (2023). UK High Pay Centre: FTSE 100 CEO:median 109:1. Public ratio disclosure has not significantly reduced ratios but has influenced shareholder voting.
**Takeaways:**
- Ratio disclosure is now standard regulatory practice.
- Say-on-pay votes are increasingly contested.
- High ratios attract reputational risk during downsizing.

## 155. Sales Compensation Plan Design
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Specialised compensation plan combining base salary and variable commission/bonus tied to sales results — typically 50/50 to 70/30 base/variable split. Designed to drive specific selling behaviours.
**Application:** Components: target total comp; mix; quota setting; commission rate; accelerators (above-quota); SPIFFs (short-term promos); territory adjustments; deal-credit rules. Plan design follows business strategy: new logo focus → new-business commission; retention focus → renewal credit.
**Case Study:** A B2B SaaS firm faced declining net retention. Diagnosis: sales comp paid full credit on new logos regardless of customer fit; no incentive for expansion or retention. Redesigned plan (2023): new logo commission cut from 10% → 7%; expansion commission introduced (8%); retention bonus for net retention >110%. Result over 18 months: net retention rose from 102% to 116%; sales rep "right-fit" deal closes rose 30%; sales rep mix shifted toward those skilled at expansion.
**Expert insight:** Alexander Group: sales comp redesign is the single highest-leverage move in revenue strategy. Mercer: 65% of sales orgs redesign plans annually; major redesigns every 3-5 years. Salesforce.com's Trailhead training programme is a benchmark.
**Takeaways:**
- Sales comp must reflect strategic priority (new vs expand vs retain).
- Quota fairness drives plan acceptance.
- Frequent over-tuning causes plan fatigue — major redesigns periodic.

## 156. Team-Based Incentives
**List:** A
**Definition:** Variable pay tied to team or unit performance rather than individual — encouraging collaboration, shared accountability, and collective outcomes.
**Application:** Forms: team bonuses; profit-sharing; gain-sharing (Scanlon, Rucker, Improshare plans); employee ownership. Fit: highly interdependent work (R&D teams, manufacturing, software). Mix with individual incentives to balance.
**Expert insight:** Lawler's research: gain-sharing plans deliver 10-20% productivity gains in manufacturing. Employee ownership (ESOPs in US, EOTs in UK) increasingly seen as alternative ownership model — John Lewis Partnership, Mondragón as exemplars.
**Takeaways:**
- Best fit for interdependent work.
- Mix individual + team to balance signal.
- Gain-sharing requires transparent metrics and trust.

## 157. Skills-Based / Competency-Based Pay
**List:** A
**Definition:** Pay structure based on demonstrated skills or competencies rather than job title or grade — employees earn more by acquiring/demonstrating specified capabilities.
**Application:** Skills certification process; pay increments per skill milestone; broad-banding to accommodate skill-based progression. Fits roles where skills determine value (technology, manufacturing). Less suitable for managerial or hierarchical roles.
**Expert insight:** Lawler's research: skills-based pay improves productivity in skill-intensive operations. Adoption stalled in 1990s-2000s but reviving with skills-based hiring trend. Deloitte Future of Work: skills-based organisations growing 53% faster.
**Takeaways:**
- Requires robust skills assessment/certification process.
- Fits skill-intensive operations best.
- Resurging interest with skills-based hiring movement.

## 159. ACA Employer Mandate — US
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Affordable Care Act provision requiring "Applicable Large Employers" (50+ FTEs) to offer affordable, minimum-value health coverage to 95%+ of full-time employees or face penalties under IRC §4980H.
**Application:** Compliance: track FTE counts; offer compliant coverage to FT employees (30+ hours/week); collect dependent-coverage info; annual 1095-C reporting; affordability test (employee contribution ≤ 9.12% of household income 2023). Penalties: ~$2,970/FTE (2024 "A penalty"); ~$4,460/non-compliant offer (2024 "B penalty").
**Case Study:** A US restaurant chain (1,200 locations, 22,000 employees, ~12,000 FT-equivalent) faced 1094-C reporting errors and a proposed IRS Letter 226-J assessment of $14M. Investigation revealed: variable-hour employees miscategorised; affordability test miscalculated for tipped workers. Remediation: (1) implemented ACA-compliant time-tracking; (2) hired specialised ACA reporting vendor; (3) restructured FT-eligible workforce; (4) negotiated reduced penalty of $2.4M with IRS. Ongoing annual compliance now monitored monthly.
**Expert insight:** SHRM: 31% of ALEs received Letter 226-J at some point. Software vendors (e.g., HRLogics, Equifax ACA Reporting) have become essential. Tipped/variable-hour workforces face highest compliance risk.
**Takeaways:**
- Tracking FT-equivalent hours is the compliance foundation.
- 1094-C/1095-C reporting errors are common and costly.
- Specialised vendors usually pay for themselves through risk reduction.

## 160. COBRA Administration
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US federal law (Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act 1985) requiring employers with 20+ employees to offer continued group health coverage to employees and dependents after qualifying events (termination, divorce, etc.) for 18-36 months.
**Application:** Employer obligations: notify of COBRA rights within 14 days of qualifying event; collect 102% of premium from electing individuals; manage enrollment, payments, communications. Compliance failures: $110/day per qualified beneficiary penalty + tax penalties.
**Case Study:** A US manufacturer outsourced COBRA admin to a vendor in 2018. In 2021, an internal audit found 240 missed notifications over 3 years — including 18 employees who later had medical events without coverage. Litigation exposure: $12M+. Investigation: vendor's outdated census files. Remediation: (1) brought COBRA in-house; (2) deployed COBRA software (Discovery Benefits, BASIC); (3) automated notification triggers from HRIS; (4) settled exposure for $3.4M. Ongoing audit cycle implemented.
**Expert insight:** SHRM: COBRA litigation has grown 320% since 2018, often via deficient-notice class actions. Specialised vendors are standard but require oversight. ARRA temporary COBRA subsidies (2009, 2021) created additional complexity.
**Takeaways:**
- COBRA outsourcing requires active vendor management.
- Census/event triggering from HRIS is essential.
- Deficient-notice class actions are growing rapidly.

## 161. Cafeteria/Flexible Benefits — Section 125
**List:** A
**Definition:** Tax-advantaged benefits structures (IRC Section 125 in US) allowing employees to choose pre-tax benefits — including FSAs (medical, dependent care), HSAs, premium-only plans, and commuter benefits.
**Application:** Standard offerings: Premium Only Plan (POP) for pre-tax health premiums; Flexible Spending Accounts ($3,200/year medical 2024); Dependent Care FSA ($5,000/year); commuter benefits ($315/month parking/transit 2024). HSA requires HDHP enrollment.
**Expert insight:** WorldatWork: 89% of US employers offer some Section 125 plan. ADP: average employee saves $1,200-$2,500/year via FSA/HSA pre-tax election. UK equivalent (salary sacrifice) operates similarly for pensions, cycle-to-work, and childcare.
**Takeaways:**
- Significant employee value at minimal employer cost.
- Communication drives utilisation rates.
- HSA + HDHP combo is highest-value option.

## 162. Pension Auto-Enrolment UK & Retirement Plans (DB/DC/401(k))
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** UK Auto-Enrolment (Pensions Act 2008) requires employers to automatically enrol eligible workers in a qualifying pension scheme with minimum 8% combined contribution (3% employer minimum). US 401(k) plans = defined-contribution retirement plans; DB plans = defined-benefit (largely closed in private sector).
**Application:** UK obligations: re-enrol every 3 years; provide info; ongoing contribution. US: 401(k) plan design (vendor, investment menu, employer match, auto-enrolment under SECURE Act 2.0); ERISA fiduciary compliance.
**Case Study:** A UK retailer (8,400 employees) was assessed £92K penalty by The Pensions Regulator for late re-enrolment in 2019. Root cause: payroll process didn't trigger 3-year re-enrolment cycle. Remediation: (1) calendar-based compliance triggers in HRIS; (2) annual TPR assessment; (3) trustee oversight strengthened; (4) communication campaign — opt-out rate fell from 14% to 6%. Cumulative impact: £4.2M additional pension contributions across workforce; significantly improved long-term retention.
**Expert insight:** TPR: Auto-enrolment has increased UK workplace pension participation from 47% (2012) to 88% (2023). DOL/EBSA enforces ERISA in US. Vanguard, Fidelity dominate US 401(k) recordkeeping.
**Takeaways:**
- Calendar compliance triggers prevent penalty risk.
- Communication campaigns reduce opt-outs.
- Investment menu design (target-date funds) drives outcomes.

## 163. ERISA & Fiduciary Responsibilities
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US Employee Retirement Income Security Act (1974) governs private sector retirement and health benefit plans. Imposes fiduciary duties: prudent expert standard, exclusive purpose, diversification, plan documents.
**Application:** Fiduciary obligations: select investments prudently; monitor fees; provide required disclosures; act in plan participants' interest. Litigation exposure: excessive fee suits, imprudent fund selection, fiduciary breach.
**Case Study:** A US Fortune 500 firm's 401(k) plan ($2.1B AUM) was sued in 2020 for excessive fees and imprudent fund selection (high-fee actively managed funds when comparable index funds were available). Settlement: $9.5M plus fee restructuring. Lesson: fiduciary committee replaced with independent advisor; quarterly fee benchmarking; investment policy statement strengthened; participant education enhanced.
**Expert insight:** ERISA litigation has surged — 2022 set record with 200+ excessive fee cases. ERISA Industry Committee (ERIC) tracks compliance. DOL ESG fiduciary rule (2022) added climate considerations.
**Takeaways:**
- Fiduciary process is everything in defending claims.
- Index funds + low fees is the strongest defence position.
- Document the prudent process at every committee meeting.

## 165. Equal Pay — Like Work / Equal Value
**List:** A
**Definition:** Right to equal pay for equal work between men and women (UK Equality Act 2010 ss. 64-83). Three bases: Like Work (substantially same), Work Rated as Equivalent (job evaluation), Work of Equal Value (analytical comparison).
**Application:** Defence requires "Genuine Material Factor" not related to sex — e.g., experience, location, market rate. UK landmark cases: Asda v Brierley (warehouse vs store); Birmingham City Council (bonuses to male manual workers). Analytical job evaluation provides strongest defence.
**Expert insight:** UK Asda case (Supreme Court 2021): allows shop-floor workers to compare to distribution centre workers. Pay equity claims now £multi-million liability for large UK retailers. EHRC enforces.
**Takeaways:**
- Pay disparities by occupational segregation are visible — manage proactively.
- Job evaluation is the strongest legal defence.
- Equal Value claims allow surprising comparators.

## 166. Gender Pay Gap Reporting — UK

**Application:** Snapshot date: 5 April; report by 4 April following year on gov.uk and own website. Most firms include narrative explaining gap and actions. Common drivers: occupational segregation, vertical segregation (fewer women in senior roles), part-time concentration.

**Takeaways:**
- Reporting without action plan signals tokenism.
- Quartile distribution often more revealing than headline number.
- Voluntary ethnicity pay gap reporting becoming standard.

## 167. Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US 2009 law extending the statute of limitations for filing equal-pay discrimination claims — each discriminatory paycheck restarts the 180-day filing window (overturning Supreme Court's *Ledbetter v Goodyear* 2007).
**Application:** Impact: individuals can claim equal pay violations on current paychecks even if discrimination started decades ago. Tightens employer obligations on ongoing pay-equity monitoring. Pairs with EPA, Title VII, ADEA.
**Case Study:** Goodyear's tire plant supervisor Lilly Ledbetter discovered through anonymous note that male peers were paid significantly more. Sued in 1998; jury awarded $3.8M. Reversed in Supreme Court 2007 on statute of limitations grounds (claim must have been filed within 180 days of original discriminatory decision). Public outcry led to Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act 2009 — first law signed by President Obama. Practical lesson for employers: pay equity issues now have indefinite legal life — proactive audits are essential.
**Expert insight:** EEOC enforcement of Equal Pay Act has intensified post-Ledbetter. Pay transparency laws (NY, CA, CO, WA) further increase litigation risk. Mercer: pay equity audits now standard practice in Fortune 500.
**Takeaways:**
- Pay discrimination has indefinite legal life under Ledbetter.
- Proactive pay equity audits are essential risk mitigation.
- Each paycheck is a new violation — ongoing remediation required.

- Transparency laws are accelerating globally.

## 170. FLSA Exempt vs Non-Exempt Classification
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US Fair Labor Standards Act (1938) classification: exempt employees (not entitled to overtime) must meet salary basis test ($684/week or $35,568/year minimum 2024; proposed $58,656 by 2025) AND duties test (executive, administrative, professional, outside sales, computer).
**Application:** Misclassification risk: paying as "exempt" when employee fails duties test triggers back-wage claims for 2-3 years overtime + liquidated damages + attorney fees. Key tests: independent discretion, primary duty, supervisory functions.
**Case Study:** A US tech firm classified 84 "Engineering Managers" as exempt. Audit revealed 38 spent <50% of time on management (primary duty = engineering). Back-wage exposure (2 years): $4.8M; liquidated damages: $4.8M; attorney fees: $1.2M. DOL settled at $7.2M. Reclassification, new role design, and time-tracking implementation followed. Lesson: title doesn't determine status — duties do.
**Expert insight:** DOL's 2024 proposed rule would raise threshold to $58,656, sweeping in millions of currently-exempt workers. Misclassification claims surged 240% post-2016 (last threshold change). SHRM: 23% of US employers audit classification annually.
**Takeaways:**
- Duties test trumps title or salary level.
- Annual classification audit is best practice.
- 2025 threshold change will require widespread reclassification.

## 171. National Minimum Wage / National Living Wage
**List:** A

## 182. Wrongful Termination & Constructive Dismissal

## 183. Unfair Dismissal UK — Fair Reasons (CCRSS)
**List:** A
**Definition:** UK Employment Rights Act 1996: dismissal is unfair unless employer proves a "potentially fair reason": Capability, Conduct, Redundancy, Statutory bar (illegality), or Some Other Substantial Reason. Procedure must also be reasonable.
**Application:** Even with fair reason, dismissal can be unfair if procedure inadequate (ACAS Code). 2-year qualifying service required for ordinary unfair dismissal claims. Automatic unfair dismissal categories (pregnancy, whistleblowing, TU activity) apply from day 1.
**Expert insight:** ACAS uplift up to 25% for non-compliant procedure. Average UK unfair dismissal award £8K, but discrimination uplift can exceed £100K. CIPD: 21% of UK organisations have lost an Employment Tribunal claim in past 3 years.
**Takeaways:**
- Both substantive (reason) and procedural fairness required.
- Day-1 protections override 2-year qualifying period.
- Procedure failures are the most common defeat.

## 184. Band of Reasonable Responses Test
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** UK tribunal test for unfair dismissal: was the employer's decision within the range of responses a reasonable employer could have made? Not whether the tribunal would have dismissed.
**Application:** Tribunals do not substitute their own view; they assess whether the dismissal fell within reasonable employer range. Procedural fairness, consistency of treatment, and severity of misconduct are weighed. Established in *British Leyland v Swift* (1981); *Iceland Frozen Foods v Jones* (1982).
**Case Study:** Mr X, a 12-year warehouse employee, was summarily dismissed for theft of £8 worth of stock. He claimed unfair dismissal. Tribunal found: reason (conduct/theft) was fair; investigation reasonable; procedure compliant. Question: was dismissal within reasonable responses range for £8 theft? Tribunal held YES — clear policy, theft regardless of value, consistent application. Compare: Mr Y, 25-year employee, dismissed for £2 cookie theft; tribunal found dismissal outside reasonable range given long service and minor value — alternative response (final written warning) was within range. Both decisions defensible on facts.
**Expert insight:** *Foley v Post Office* (2000) confirmed test. Recent ECHR interaction with band-of-reasonable-responses creates some uncertainty around proportionality.
**Takeaways:**
- Tribunal asks "reasonable", not "right."
- Consistency of treatment across cases is critical.
- Long service, mitigating factors affect "reasonableness."

## 185. Redundancy Law UK — Consultation, Selection
**List:** A
**Definition:** UK redundancy law (Employment Rights Act 1996, TULRCA 1992): dismissal due to closure or diminished requirement. Requires fair selection criteria, individual/collective consultation, and statutory redundancy pay.
**Application:** Collective consultation thresholds: 20+ in 90 days → 30 days; 100+ → 45 days. Information & consultation duties under TULRCA. Selection criteria: objective, non-discriminatory (LIFO discouraged). Statutory redundancy pay formula: 0.5/1/1.5 week's pay × years × age multipliers.
**Expert insight:** UK Supreme Court *USDAW v Ethel Austin* (2015): collective consultation triggers per establishment. CIPD: 67% of UK redundancies legally challenged result from process flaws, not substance. Specialist legal support essential.
**Takeaways:**
- Collective consultation thresholds trigger strict timelines.
- Selection criteria must be objective and consistently applied.
- Statutory minima are floor, not ceiling.

## 186. WARN Act — US
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act 1988: employers with 100+ employees must give 60 days' notice of plant closings (50+ employees) or mass layoffs (33% of workforce or 500+ employees). Some state mini-WARN laws stricter (CA WARN: 75+ employers).
**Application:** Notice to: affected employees, union, state dislocated worker unit, chief local elected official. Exceptions: faltering company, unforeseeable business circumstances, natural disaster (each narrowly construed). Penalty: 60 days back pay + benefits.
**Case Study:** A US tech firm announced 2,400 layoffs on Day-0 with 24 hours notice in 2023. WARN Act class action filed within 7 days. Defence claimed "unforeseeable business circumstances" — judge rejected as conditions were known months prior. Settlement: $34M (60 days pay × 2,400 employees + attorney fees). Lesson: WARN notice timeline must drive layoff planning, not the reverse.
**Expert insight:** WARN class actions surged in 2022-2023 tech layoff wave. CA mini-WARN further extends to 75-employee threshold. Lyft, Twitter, others faced WARN claims. ML's Layoff Tracker monitors.
**Takeaways:**
- 60 days notice is non-negotiable except narrow exceptions.
- State mini-WARN laws may apply additionally.
- "Unforeseeable" defence is very narrowly construed.

## 187. TUPE Transfers & ETO Defence
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** UK Transfer of Undertakings (Protection of Employment) Regulations 2006: when business or service is transferred, employees' contracts transfer with them automatically. Dismissals related to transfer are automatically unfair unless ETO (Economic, Technical, or Organisational) reason applies.
**Application:** Applies to: business sales, mergers, outsourcing, in-sourcing, service provision changes. Obligations: inform & consult; transfer terms preserved; dismissals connected to transfer = automatically unfair unless ETO defence. Harmonisation of terms post-transfer is difficult.
**Case Study:** A UK local authority outsourced its IT function (52 staff) to an external provider in 2021. Provider sought to harmonise pay/benefits (lower than authority terms) within 6 months. 18 employees raised TUPE claims. Tribunal held: harmonisation = dismissal & re-engagement on new terms; ETO defence rejected (no organisational restructuring or technical change). Award: £610K plus reinstatement of terms. Provider's lesson: TUPE-protected terms transfer permanently unless genuine ETO reason justifies change.
**Expert insight:** UK Court of Appeal *Berriman v Delabole Slate* established ETO requires changes "to the workforce" not just to terms. CIPD: TUPE remains the most complex UK employment law area for HR practitioners.
**Takeaways:**
- TUPE protects T&Cs at transfer indefinitely.
- ETO defence is narrow — workforce changes required.
- Pre-transfer due diligence on T&Cs is critical.

## 188. Separation Agreements
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Contractual agreements between employer and departing employee — typically including severance payment in exchange for release of claims, confidentiality, non-disparagement, restrictive covenants.

Settlement: revised agreements; restored opportunity to sign; compensation for those who declined and missed out. Lesson: post-2023 NLRB McLaren Macomb decision narrowed acceptable severance provisions on confidentiality and non-disparagement.
**Expert insight:** NLRB *McLaren Macomb* (2023): broad non-disparagement and confidentiality clauses violate NLRA in US. ACAS: UK settlement agreements require independent legal advice to be binding. EEOC OWBPA strictly enforced for age 40+ workers.
**Takeaways:**
- Post-McLaren: confidentiality clauses must be narrowly drafted in US.
- UK requires independent legal advice for binding settlements.
- Releases are unenforceable for whistleblower claims, etc.

## 191. Frames of Reference — Unitarist, Pluralist, Radical/Marxist
**List:** A
**Definition:** Alan Fox's (1966) three perspectives on the employment relationship: Unitarist (shared interests, conflict is dysfunction); Pluralist (legitimate competing interests, managed conflict); Radical/Marxist (structural conflict rooted in capital-labour power imbalance).
**Application:** Each frame implies different HR practices: Unitarist → team-building, shared values, single source of authority; Pluralist → collective bargaining, joint consultation, partnership; Radical → critical analysis of power, voice for marginalised. Most managers operate Unitarist; ER academics often Pluralist.
**Expert insight:** Edmund Heery and Mick Marchington have developed pluralist theory. CIPD largely operates pluralist frame (Level 7 EER). Radical frame less commonly applied but valuable for critical analysis of power.
**Takeaways:**
- Frames shape what counts as "the problem."
- Pluralist frame fits unionised/representative environments.
- Critical analysis (radical) surfaces hidden power dynamics.

## 192. Employer Strategies Toward Unions
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Range of employer postures: Suppression (active opposition, union-busting); Substitution (replacing union with comparable benefits/voice); Partnership (recognised union + collaborative relationship); Avoidance (compliance-only). Most firms blend approaches.
**Application:** Strategy choice driven by: management philosophy; sector tradition; legal context (UK has statutory recognition; US allows more aggressive opposition); cost considerations. Partnership approaches typically yield better outcomes long-term.
**Case Study:** A UK retailer responded to USDAW recognition campaign in 2018 with substitution strategy: enhanced pay (above-market), structured voice (works council, employee forums), strong benefits. Union withdrew application. Sustainability: 5 years later, voice mechanisms maintained, no further union campaigns; staff engagement 14 points above sector. Compare US firm that suppressed organising: 30% turnover; NLRB ULP findings; brand damage; eventual recognition forced. Substitution costs > suppression costs > partnership in long run.
**Expert insight:** Edmund Heery: "Strategic choice" of frame is increasingly explicit. Starbucks 2021-2024 union campaign is notable US case study. Walmart's history is classic suppression. John Lewis Partnership exemplifies enlightened employee involvement.
**Takeaways:**
- Suppression brand-damages and rarely sustainable.
- Substitution requires sustained voice investment.
- Partnership most sustainable but requires trust.

## 193. NLRA & Section 7 Rights
**List:** A
**Definition:** US National Labor Relations Act (1935 "Wagner Act"): Section 7 protects employee rights to self-organise, form/join unions, bargain collectively, and engage in concerted activity for mutual aid/protection.
**Application:** Section 7 protects even non-union employees in concerted activity (discussing pay, working conditions). Section 8 prohibits employer unfair labor practices (interference, domination, discrimination, retaliation). NLRB enforces.
**Expert insight:** NLRB *Stericycle* (2023) tightened restrictions on overly-broad employer policies (handbooks, social media). NLRB *McLaren Macomb* (2023) on severance agreement provisions. AFL-CIO and major unions advocate PRO Act extending protections.
**Takeaways:**
- Section 7 protects non-union employees too.
- Handbook policies must be carefully drafted post-Stericycle.
- "Concerted activity" includes social media discussions of pay.

## 195. Union Organising & Election Process
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Process by which employees form a union: card-signing campaign (30%+ for petition; 50%+ for card-check claim in some jurisdictions) → NLRB petition → election (typically 6-8 weeks) → certification if majority votes in favour.
**Application:** Employer permissible: factual information; opinion (must not threaten/promise); election day procedures. Employer impermissible: interrogating; threatening; surveilling; promising benefits; firing organisers. Most contested elections have ULP charges.

Starbucks faced 500+ ULP charges (allegations of firings, store closures, surveillance). NLRB orders for reinstatement, bargaining orders. Lesson for HR: union-avoidance through suppression costs more than addressing the underlying issues that fuel organising (wages, scheduling, voice).
**Expert insight:** Starbucks campaign reshaped US organising momentum. Cornell ILR School tracks data. NLRB 2024 Cemex decision: bargaining order remedy where ULPs prevent fair election.
**Takeaways:**
- Suppression strategies rarely succeed long-term.
- Worker concerns precede union — address proactively.
- Recent NLRB rulings expand remedies.

## 196. Certification & Decertification
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Certification = NLRB-formal recognition of union as bargaining representative after election. Decertification = formal removal — requires 30% employee petition, then majority vote against representation.
**Application:** Decertification petitions: filed by employees (not employer); typically within 30-60 day window before contract expiration. Employer cannot initiate but can provide neutral support. "Open period" timing critical.
**Case Study:** A US manufacturer of 320 employees was unionised in 2018 (52-48 vote). Over 5 years, union failed to deliver promised gains; meeting attendance dropped. In 2023, 40% of employees signed decertification petition. Election: 71% voted to decertify. Lessons: poor union representation often leads to decertification; employer's role was neutral throughout (legal requirement); transparent communication on company benefits was permissible.
**Expert insight:** NLRB: decertification petitions average 200-300 annually in US. Trends fluctuate with union performance and economic conditions.
**Takeaways:**
- Employer cannot initiate decertification.
- "Open period" timing is procedurally crucial.
- Union performance drives decertification more than employer activity.

## 197. Collective Bargaining — Mandatory/Permissive/Illegal Subjects
**List:** A
**Definition:** Subjects of bargaining classified by NLRA: Mandatory (wages, hours, working conditions — must be bargained on demand); Permissive (other subjects, can decline to bargain); Illegal (cannot be bargained).
**Application:** Mandatory examples: wages, benefits, hours, safety, grievance procedure, layoffs, vacation, sick leave. Permissive: corporate strategy, plant location, products. Illegal: closed shop (US), discriminatory provisions.
**Expert insight:** NLRB *Borg-Warner* (1958) established categories. Outsourcing decisions: NLRB *Fibreboard* held some outsourcing mandatory. UK equivalent: collective agreements via TULRCA recognise certain matters.
**Takeaways:**
- Classification of subjects is contentious — case law evolves.
- Refusing to bargain on mandatory subjects = ULP.
- Outsourcing decisions may trigger duty to bargain.

## 198. Single/Multi-Employer Bargaining
**List:** A
**Definition:** Single-employer bargaining: union negotiates with one employer (one contract). Multi-employer: union negotiates one contract covering multiple employers (e.g., construction industry, supermarket sector).

**Takeaways:**
- Multi-employer levels sectoral playing field.
- Single-employer enables strategic differentiation.

## 199. Collective Agreements — Legal Status
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Agreements between employer and recognised union. UK: presumed not legally binding between parties (TULRCA s.179) unless explicitly stated; individual terms incorporated into employment contracts. US: collective bargaining agreements (CBAs) legally enforceable.
**Application:** UK collective agreement → incorporated terms become contractual rights enforceable by individuals. US CBA → enforceable in federal court; grievance arbitration the standard remedy.
**Case Study:** A UK manufacturer's recognised trade union agreed to a 3-year pay deal in 2022 (3.5% per year). Inflation hit 11% in 2023; union demanded reopening. Employer refused, citing 3-year agreement. Union balloted for industrial action. Mediation eventually produced one-time lump sum payments. Key learning: collective agreements include both legally-binding individual terms (pay rates) and non-binding union-employer commitments (3-year duration). Disputes during agreement period have limited legal remedy.
**Expert insight:** UK CBI/TUC discuss strengthening collective agreement enforceability. US: NLRB and federal courts enforce CBAs via §301 Labor Management Relations Act.
**Takeaways:**
- UK collective agreements not directly binding union-employer.
- Individual terms (pay) are contractually binding via incorporation.
- US CBAs fully enforceable.

## 200. Duty to Bargain in Good Faith
**List:** A
**Definition:** NLRA §8(d) requires both parties to bargain in good faith — meeting at reasonable times, sharing information, considering proposals, but no requirement to reach agreement.
**Application:** Good-faith indicators: meeting regularly; making concessions; sharing relevant info; serious engagement. Bad-faith indicators: surface bargaining; rigid pre-conditions; bypassing the union; predatory tactics. ULP charge possible for either side.
**Expert insight:** Recent NLRB enforcement intensified — Cemex (2023) decision allows bargaining orders without elections when ULPs taint process. CIPD: UK equivalent duty applies under TULRCA but enforcement weaker.
**Takeaways:**
- Good faith is process, not outcome.
- Surface bargaining is hard to prove but pursued.
- Information sharing is the most-litigated dimension.

## 202. Taft-Hartley & Landrum-Griffin Acts
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Taft-Hartley Act (1947): amended NLRA to restrict union powers — outlawed closed shops, allowed state right-to-work laws, prohibited certain union ULPs. Landrum-Griffin (1959): union democracy requirements, fiduciary duties of union officers.
**Application:** Right-to-work states (27 US states): employees in unionised workplaces cannot be required to pay union dues. Union financial reporting (LM-2 forms) required under Landrum-Griffin. Union elections governed by Title IV.
**Case Study:** A union officer in 2019 was prosecuted under Landrum-Griffin for embezzlement of $230K in union funds. Conviction: 5 years federal prison + restitution. Union placed under federal monitoring. Investigation by Office of Labor-Management Standards (OLMS). Lesson: union fiduciary duties are heavily enforced; transparency required via LM forms.
**Expert insight:** DOL OLMS publishes union financial reports publicly. PRO Act would repeal right-to-work provisions if enacted.
**Takeaways:**
- Right-to-work laws weaken union finances.
- Landrum-Griffin requires extensive transparency.
- Union fiduciary breaches federally prosecuted.

## 203. TULRCA 1992 — UK
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Trade Union and Labour Relations (Consolidation) Act 1992: principal UK trade union legislation — consolidating prior law on union recognition, collective bargaining, industrial action ballots, redundancy consultation.
**Application:** Key provisions: statutory recognition procedure (Schedule A1); right to be accompanied; collective consultation duties (s.188); strike ballot requirements (s.226); protection from blacklisting.
**Case Study:** A UK transport firm failed to comply with s.188 collective consultation (20+ redundancies, 30-day consultation required). Skipped to 7-day notice. Union claim: protective awards (90 days pay × 250 employees). Tribunal awarded full 90 days = £4.1M. Lesson: TULRCA collective consultation timelines are strict; partial compliance is not partial penalty — full 90-day award likely.
**Expert insight:** UK Employment Lawyers Association (ELA) tracks TULRCA case law. Trade Union Act 2016 amended TULRCA to require higher strike ballot thresholds.
**Takeaways:**
- Collective consultation timelines are absolute.
- Protective awards are uncapped exposure.
- Strike ballot requirements have hardened.

## 204. Statutory Union Recognition — CAC UK
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** UK procedure for unions to win automatic recognition: application to Central Arbitration Committee (CAC); requires 10% union membership in bargaining unit; CAC may grant recognition via card check (>50% membership) or ballot.
**Application:** Applies to employers with 21+ workers. Voluntary recognition usually negotiated first. CAC determines bargaining unit. If granted, 30-month method-of-bargaining period; collective bargaining required on pay, hours, holidays.
**Case Study:** A UK tech firm of 380 staff faced GMB union recognition application in 2020. 45% membership levels. Voluntary recognition discussions broke down. CAC ordered ballot — union won 58-42. Recognition granted; bargaining commenced on pay, holidays, hours. Within 2 years: pay structure refreshed, holiday policy harmonised. CHRO's reflection: "Voluntary recognition would have given us better terms than statutory imposition."
**Expert insight:** CAC: ~150 recognition cases annually in UK; ~70% result in recognition. Major decisions include *Boots* (2007) and *Uber* (2022).
**Takeaways:**
- Voluntary recognition negotiation almost always better than statutory.
- 10% threshold triggers process; 50% triggers card-check option.
- Bargaining unit definition is contested ground.

## 205. Works Councils — UK, EU, EWCs
**List:** A
**Definition:** Works Councils: legally-mandated employee representative bodies. UK: optional via Information and Consultation Regulations 2004 (10% workforce request triggers). EU: required in member states for 50+ employees. EWCs: European Works Councils for multinationals with 1,000+ EU employees.
**Application:** Information and consultation duties on: business performance, employment situation, decisions likely to lead to substantial changes. Distinct from collective bargaining — focus on dialogue.

**Takeaways:**
- EU/EWC obligations significant for multinationals.
- Works councils provide dialogue without collective bargaining.
- UK ICE Regs underused but rights available.

## 206. ICE Regulations 2004 — UK
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** UK Information and Consultation of Employees Regulations 2004: implement EU Directive 2002/14/EC. Employers with 50+ employees must inform and consult on workforce matters when 10% of employees request it.
**Application:** Information topics: recent and probable development of activities and economic situation. Consultation topics: employment situation, decisions likely to lead to substantial changes. Pre-existing voluntary agreement may satisfy.
**Case Study:** A UK financial services firm of 600 employees faced ICE Regulation request in 2021 (workforce concerns about return-to-office mandates). Employer engaged proactively: established 12-person employee forum, monthly meetings, agreed terms of reference. Outcome: hybrid working policy co-designed; engagement scores rose 18 points. CHRO's view: ICE Regulations became an asset, not a compliance burden, when embraced as voice mechanism.
**Expert insight:** ACAS guidance: ICE Regs underused but valuable. CIPD: 11% of eligible UK employers have ICE-compliant arrangements; uptake post-COVID rising.
**Takeaways:**
- 10% trigger threshold often misunderstood.
- Voluntary forum can satisfy obligation.
- Embracing voice mechanism beats minimum compliance.

## 207. Employee Voice Tools
**List:** A
**Definition:** Mechanisms for employees to express views, contribute to decisions, and influence the workplace. Range: individual (suggestion schemes, 1:1s), collective (forums, councils), representative (unions).

## 208. Involvement vs Participation
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Involvement = employer-initiated, task-focused engagement (e.g., briefings, suggestion schemes). Participation = shared decision-making, structural (e.g., works councils, board representation, co-determination).
**Application:** Involvement is most common in UK private sector (high-volume, low-influence). Participation more developed in continental Europe (works councils, co-determination). Choice affects power-sharing, decision speed, and engagement.
**Case Study:** A UK retailer experimented with deeper participation: store managers given P&L authority, profit-share, voting rights on store-level decisions. Engagement rose 32 points; retention improved 18%; store profitability rose 11%. But: speed of company-wide decision making slowed. Hybrid model adopted: stores autonomous on operations, central on strategy. Lesson: participation has costs and benefits — design intentionally.
**Expert insight:** Marchington's research distinguishes high/low influence and direct/representative. CIPD: participation correlates with engagement but requires culture support.
**Takeaways:**
- Involvement is engagement-lite; participation is power-sharing.
- Participation requires culture and capability.
- Hybrid approaches often most practical.

## 209. Employee Communications Strategy

**Application:** Components: stakeholder map; channel matrix (email, intranet, town halls, manager cascades, video, Slack/Teams); message house; cadence calendar; measurement (open rates, sentiment, feedback). Manager cascade is the highest-trust channel.

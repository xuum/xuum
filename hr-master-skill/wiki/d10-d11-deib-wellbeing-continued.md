# HR Wiki — Domain 10-11 Continuation (Concepts 226-261)

## 226. Digital Accessibility & WCAG
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2): international standards for digital accessibility. Four principles: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust. Three conformance levels: A, AA (legal standard), AAA.
**Application:** HR digital systems (HRIS, careers sites, e-learning, intranet) must be WCAG AA-compliant: alt text on images; keyboard navigation; screen reader compatibility; colour contrast 4.5:1; captions on video. ADA Title III, UK Equality Act 2010 (Section 20), and EU Accessibility Act 2025 enforce.
**Case Study:** A US bank's careers site failed accessibility scan by NFB (National Federation of the Blind). Class action alleged blind applicants couldn't apply via screen reader. Settlement: $400K + comprehensive remediation. Lesson: accessibility is no longer optional. Remediation programme: WCAG 2.2 AA across all HR digital systems; accessibility testing in vendor selection; quarterly audits; staff training. Cost: $1.8M over 18 months; risk avoided: $multi-million class action exposure.
**Expert insight:** US WebAIM Million 2023 Report: 96.3% of homepages have detectable WCAG failures. Section 508 (federal contractors) and ADA Title III lawsuits surged (3,500+ in 2023). EU Accessibility Act effective June 2025.
**Takeaways:**
- Accessibility is legal compliance + ethical imperative.
- Test with assistive tech, not just automated tools.
- Vendor selection must include accessibility.

## 227. Pay Transparency as Inclusion Lever
**List:** A
**Definition:** Disclosure of salary information — in job postings, internally between peers, in reports — as a tool for surfacing and addressing pay inequities, especially affecting underrepresented groups.
**Application:** Implementation: salary ranges in job ads (now legally required in NY, CA, CO, WA); peer pay sharing permitted (US NLRA Section 7); aggregate pay equity reports. Aligns with EU Pay Transparency Directive (2026): mandatory pay-related information at hire, in role, on request.
**Expert insight:** Trusaic, Salary.com data show pay transparency in postings reduces gender pay gap by 7-12% in compliant jurisdictions. PayScale research: 89% of organisations cite transparency as a 2024 priority.
**Takeaways:**
- Transparency surfaces inequities — preparation is critical.
- Peer-pay sharing protected under US NLRA.
- EU Directive will force standardisation 2026.

## 228. Employee Resource Groups — ERGs
**List:** A
**Definition:** Voluntary, employee-led groups organised around shared identity, interest, or experience (e.g., women in tech, LGBTQ+, racial/ethnic groups, neurodivergent, parents, veterans). Provide community, voice, and business contributions.
**Application:** Lifecycle: charter; sponsor; budget; events; metrics. Modern ERGs increasingly involve business contribution: input to product (e.g., disability ERG reviewing accessibility); recruiting (referrals, employer brand events); culture (allyship training). Executive sponsorship critical.
**Expert insight:** McKinsey D&I research: high-ERG organisations have 2x retention among target groups. Catalyst: 96% of Fortune 500 have ERGs. Modern critique: avoid "tokenism" — ERGs need real resources, voice, and impact.
**Takeaways:**
- Executive sponsor is the most important factor.
- ERGs without business influence become social clubs.
- Budget signals seriousness (typically $1K-$10K per ERG).

## 229. Skills-First / Bias-Reduced Hiring
**List:** A
**Definition:** Hiring approach prioritising demonstrated skills over traditional credentials (degrees, prior employer brand) — combined with structured selection methods reducing bias (structured interviews, blind screening, work samples).
**Application:** Practices: remove degree requirements where not essential; rephrase JD requirements as skills; use structured behavioural interviews (STAR); blind initial screening (remove names, photos); standardised scoring rubrics; diverse interview panels.
**Expert insight:** WEF Future of Jobs 2023: skills-based hiring is the #1 growing HR practice. IBM, Accenture, Google removed many degree requirements. McKinsey: structured interviews reduce bias 50% vs unstructured. Bersin: skills-based organisations grow 53% faster.
**Takeaways:**
- Degree requirements often unjustified — audit annually.
- Structured interview = same questions, same rubric, all candidates.
- Diverse panels reduce affinity bias.

## 230. Measuring Inclusion & Belonging Outcomes
**List:** A
**Definition:** Quantitative and qualitative measurement of inclusion (fair treatment, voice) and belonging (sense of fit, psychological safety). Distinct from diversity (representation) and equity (fair processes).
**Application:** Metrics: belonging scale items (e.g., "I can be myself at work"); psychological safety scale (Edmondson 7-item); attrition by demographic; promotion rates by demographic; engagement by demographic; ERG participation; concerns raised. Use mixed methods: surveys + listening sessions + analytics.
**Expert insight:** Belonging research (Cobb-Clark, Catalyst, EY): belonging predicts retention and discretionary effort 4x better than diversity numbers alone. EY's Belonging Barometer: 39% of US workers report high belonging — significantly lower for underrepresented groups.
**Takeaways:**
- Belonging is the outcome; diversity is the input.
- Cut metrics by demographic to surface gaps.
- Combine quant + qual for full picture.

## 231. Occupational Segregation — Horizontal/Vertical
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Horizontal segregation = concentration of demographic groups in specific occupations/sectors (e.g., women in care work, men in engineering). Vertical segregation = concentration in lower levels of hierarchy (e.g., few women/minorities in senior management).
**Application:** Diagnose with workforce data cuts: % of role family by gender/ethnicity/disability; level distribution by demographic; pipeline analysis. Root causes: pipeline (early stages), selection bias, retention, advancement, sponsorship.
**Case Study:** A UK engineering firm analysed its workforce: 12% female overall; 8% female engineers; 4% female senior engineers; 2% female engineering directors. Pattern: both horizontal (occupation) and vertical (level) segregation. Root cause analysis: STEM pipeline (20% female engineering graduates) → recruitment bias (12% female hires) → retention gap (women leave at 3x rate at year 5) → promotion gap (men 2.4x more likely to be promoted). Intervention: revised recruiting (university partnerships, returner programmes); structured promotion process; women's leadership programme; sponsorship for senior women. 4-year results: female engineering directors rose to 11%.
**Expert insight:** UK ONS labour market data tracks segregation. CIPD: 67% of UK workforce shows occupational segregation by gender. McKinsey Women in the Workplace (annual): tracks corporate pipeline systematically.
**Takeaways:**
- Segregation has both horizontal and vertical dimensions.
- Root cause analysis reveals where to intervene.
- Multi-year programmes needed — patient capital.

## 232. Compound Disadvantage / Intersectional Inequality
**List:** A
**Definition:** Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989): the compounding of disadvantage experienced by individuals at the intersection of multiple marginalised identities (e.g., Black women face distinct disadvantage beyond either being Black or female).
**Application:** Cut workforce data intersectionally: not just by gender or race separately, but by intersections (women of colour vs white women vs men of colour). Surfaces pay gaps, promotion gaps, and experiences not visible in single-axis analysis.
**Expert insight:** McKinsey *Women in the Workplace*: Black women in US face 64¢ on dollar (vs 84¢ for white women). UK research: ethnic minority women face wider pay gap than ethnic majority women. CIPD's intersectionality guides increasingly used.
**Takeaways:**
- Single-axis diversity hides intersectional gaps.
- Black/minority women face distinct, compounded challenges.
- Programme design must address intersections explicitly.

## 233. Returner Programmes & Apprenticeships
**List:** A
**Definition:** Returner Programmes = structured re-entry programmes for those returning from career breaks (often parental, caring, illness). Apprenticeships = paid work + learning programmes (formal in UK, growing in US).
**Application:** Returner programmes: 6-12 month structured roles with development support, mentoring, retention pathway. Apprenticeships: UK levy-funded for 3-7% of wage bill; T-Levels, degree apprenticeships expanding. Both increase access for underrepresented groups.
**Expert insight:** Women Returners (UK): 750+ programmes launched since 2014. UK Apprenticeships: 750,000 starts per year. Returner programme retention typically 80%+ at 24 months (above average). McKinsey: returner programmes one of highest-ROI D&I investments.
**Takeaways:**
- Returner programmes access overlooked talent.
- Apprenticeships open routes without traditional credentials.
- Both work best with structured development and sponsorship.

## 234. Inclusive Communication & Training

## 237. JD-R Model — Bakker & Demerouti
**List:** A
**Definition:** Job Demands-Resources Model: work characteristics fall into two categories — Job Demands (physical, social, organisational requirements) and Job Resources (aspects that help achieve goals). Demands without resources cause burnout; resources buffer demands and foster engagement.
**Application:** Diagnostic tool for wellbeing/engagement: assess demands (workload, emotional labour, role ambiguity) and resources (autonomy, support, feedback, development). Imbalance signals risk. Interventions: reduce demands or increase resources.
**Expert insight:** JD-R is the most-cited modern wellbeing model. Bakker & Demerouti (2007, 2017) refined across 200+ studies. Predicts burnout (high demands + low resources) and engagement (high resources). Used widely in HR diagnostics.
**Takeaways:**
- Reduce demands or boost resources — both work.
- Resources buffer demands.
- Autonomy is the highest-value resource.

## 238. Effort-Reward Imbalance — Siegrist
**List:** A
**Definition:** Johannes Siegrist's model: chronic mismatch between high effort (workload, time pressure) and low reward (pay, esteem, security, advancement) causes stress, with health and engagement consequences.
**Application:** Diagnostic: ERI ratio = effort / (reward × overcommitment). High ratio → health risk. Interventions: pay equity; recognition; career development; psychological reward (autonomy, meaning). Particularly relevant in healthcare, education, frontline service.
**Expert insight:** Siegrist's research used in occupational health globally. ERI ratio > 1.0 correlates with 2x cardiovascular risk. CIPD wellbeing guidance references ERI.
**Takeaways:**
- Reward is not just pay — recognition matters.
- ERI predicts physical health, not just stress.
- Frontline workers face highest ERI risk.

## 239. Conservation of Resources Theory — Hobfoll
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Stevan Hobfoll's COR theory: people strive to acquire, retain, and protect resources (objects, conditions, characteristics, energies). Stress arises from threat of resource loss, actual loss, or failure to gain after investment. Resource loss spirals: initial loss triggers further loss.
**Application:** Wellbeing applications: identify resource-poor employees (low autonomy, support, security); prevent loss spirals (e.g., burnout → absence → performance issues → discipline); build resources during good times to buffer crises.
**Case Study:** A US healthcare provider's wellbeing analysis identified nurses in high-acuity units with the lowest "resource accumulation" (least PTO use, no time for development, missed meals). Following pandemic, this group showed 56% burnout vs 28% organisation-wide. Intervention: protected break time; mandatory PTO usage; resource-building development time; recognition programme. 24-month follow-up: burnout in target group fell to 31% (still high but trajectory reversed). Lesson: COR predicts who is vulnerable — invest before crisis.
**Expert insight:** Hobfoll's *Stress, Culture, and Community* (1998). COR widely cited in burnout research. Maslach Burnout Inventory aligns to COR framework.
**Takeaways:**
- Loss spirals are predictable — intervene early.
- Resource-poor employees most at risk.
- Build resources before crisis hits.

## 240. PERMA Model — Seligman
**List:** A
**Definition:** Martin Seligman's positive psychology framework: wellbeing = Positive emotion + Engagement (flow) + Relationships + Meaning + Accomplishment. Each component independently contributes; together produce flourishing.
**Application:** Workplace applications: cultivate positive culture (gratitude, recognition); design jobs for flow (challenge-skill balance, Csikszentmihalyi); foster relationships (teams, ERGs, social events); align work to meaning (purpose, values); enable accomplishment (achievable goals, progress markers).
**Expert insight:** Seligman's *Flourish* (2011) operationalised PERMA. University of Pennsylvania's positive psychology centre researches workplace applications. PERMA-W (workplace adaptation) is increasingly used in wellbeing surveys.
**Takeaways:**
- Five distinct dimensions — design for all.
- Engagement (flow) most under-addressed.
- Meaning matters increasingly to younger workers.

## 241. Stress, Burnout, Presenteeism, Absenteeism
**List:** A
**Definition:** Stress = pressure exceeding capacity to cope. Burnout = chronic stress syndrome (Maslach): emotional exhaustion + cynicism + reduced efficacy. Presenteeism = attending work while unwell, reducing productivity. Absenteeism = not attending work.
**Application:** Surveillance: track sickness absence rates; pulse stress surveys; manager observation. WHO classifies burnout as occupational phenomenon (2019). Distinction matters: burnout requires systemic intervention, not individual resilience training alone.
**Expert insight:** Christina Maslach is the foundational burnout researcher. WHO ICD-11 classifies burnout occupationally. CIPD: presenteeism 2x more common than absenteeism in UK, with greater productivity cost. Gallup: burnout costs $322B globally per year.
**Takeaways:**
- Burnout is systemic, not personal failing.
- Presenteeism more costly than absenteeism.
- Maslach Burnout Inventory is the validated measure.

## 242. Employee Assistance Programmes — EAPs
**List:** A
**Definition:** Workplace programmes offering confidential counselling and support to employees and dependents for personal, family, financial, legal, or work-related issues. Typically delivered via external providers.
**Application:** Standard EAP services: 24/7 telephone support; 4-8 counselling sessions; legal/financial referrals; manager consultation; critical incident response. Costs typically $1-2 per employee per month. Utilisation rates: average 5-8% but rising with stigma reduction.
**Expert insight:** EAPA (Employee Assistance Professional Association) accredits providers. CIPD: 79% of UK organisations offer EAP; utilisation higher when actively promoted. Magellan, Workplace Options, Lyra are leading providers.
**Takeaways:**
- Promotion drives utilisation more than provider quality.
- Manager training to refer is critical.
- Outcomes data often weak — push providers for evidence.

## 243. Occupational Health Services

## 244. Mental Health First Aid
**List:** A
**Definition:** Training programme equipping individuals to provide initial support to someone experiencing mental health issues until professional help is available. Originated in Australia 2000; now international.
**Application:** 2-day MHFA certification; trained "MHFAiders" in workplace as visible support resources. UK has MHFA England; US has MHFA USA. Increasingly seen as workplace standard, equivalent to physical first aid.
**Expert insight:** MHFA England: 600,000+ trained in UK. Evidence: trained MHFAiders demonstrate improved knowledge, attitudes, and confidence; uncertain evidence on outcomes for recipients. Best used as part of broader strategy, not standalone solution.
**Takeaways:**
- Visible MHFAiders reduce stigma.
- Training is necessary but not sufficient.
- Pair with EAP, manager training, culture work.

## 246. Line Manager Role in Wellbeing
**List:** A
**Definition:** The manager-employee relationship is the most powerful workplace lever for wellbeing — through job design, workload management, recognition, psychological safety, and support during challenges.
**Application:** Manager wellbeing capabilities: stress recognition; psychological safety; difficult conversations; signposting to support; reasonable adjustments; flexible working. Training programmes: MHFA, "Mental Health Confident Manager," signposting.

## 247. Stress Risk Assessments & Duty of Care
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** UK HSE-required process for managing work-related stress. Employers must assess risks per the HSE Management Standards (Demands, Control, Support, Relationships, Role, Change) and act on findings.
**Application:** HSE Management Standards Indicator Tool (35-item survey); team-level results; action planning; review cycle. Duty of care (common law and HSWA 1974): employer must take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable psychiatric injury.
**Case Study:** A UK financial services manager developed PTSD after sustained excessive workload (75-hour weeks, no support). Despite raising concerns to manager and HR, no action taken. Personal injury claim: £180K compensation. Court found: employer aware of stress; foreseeable injury; no reasonable steps taken; breach of duty of care. Lesson: stress complaints must be acted upon — documentation of action protects against claims.
**Expert insight:** *Walker v Northumberland CC* (1995) established UK stress injury liability. *Hatton v Sutherland* (2002) refined "foreseeability" test. HSE prosecutions for stress increased post-pandemic.
**Takeaways:**
- HSE Management Standards are the UK benchmark.
- Personal injury liability for stress is well-established.
- Action on identified stress is the protective factor.

## 248. OSHA Standards & General Duty Clause
**List:** A
**Definition:** US Occupational Safety and Health Act (1970): General Duty Clause (Section 5(a)(1)) requires employers to provide workplaces "free from recognised hazards causing death or serious physical harm." Specific OSHA standards cover specific hazards.
**Application:** Specific standards: Construction (29 CFR 1926); General Industry (1910); Maritime; Agriculture. General Duty Clause covers hazards without specific standards. Citations: serious, willful, repeat, other-than-serious. Penalties: $16K-$165K per violation (2024).
**Expert insight:** OSHA inspections: ~32K annually. Most-cited standards: Fall Protection, Hazard Communication, Respiratory Protection. National Emphasis Programs target specific industries. State-OSHA plans operate in 22 states.
**Takeaways:**
- General Duty Clause covers emerging hazards (heat, workplace violence).
- OSHA 300 logs required for 11+ employee firms.
- State plans may be stricter than federal.

## 249. UK Health and Safety at Work Act 1974
**List:** A
**Definition:** UK foundational health and safety law. Section 2: employer duty to ensure health, safety, welfare of employees "so far as is reasonably practicable." Risk assessment, training, safe systems of work, suitable equipment required.
**Application:** Implementation via Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999 (risk assessments), COSHH (hazardous substances), DSE Regulations (computer use), Manual Handling Regulations, etc. HSE enforces with Improvement Notices, Prohibition Notices, prosecutions.
**Expert insight:** HSE annual stats: ~1.8M workers report work-related illness; ~135 work-related fatalities in 2022/23. *R v Brent LBC* (2017): "reasonably practicable" balances risk vs cost/effort. IOSH and NEBOSH train professionals.
**Takeaways:**
- "So far as reasonably practicable" is core HSWA test.
- Risk assessments are foundational compliance document.
- HSE prosecution can include unlimited fines and imprisonment.

## 250. Hazard ID, Safety Management Systems, Incident Investigation
**List:** A
**Definition:** Hazard identification = systematic recognition of workplace risks. Safety Management Systems (SMS) = integrated frameworks (plan-do-check-act) for managing safety (ISO 45001, HSG65 Plan-Do-Check-Act). Incident investigation = structured root-cause analysis of accidents/near-misses.
**Application:** Hazard ID techniques: walk-throughs, JSAs, hazard registers, HAZOP. SMS: policy, planning, implementation, monitoring, review. Investigation: TapRooT, 5-Whys, Fishbone, Reason's Swiss Cheese Model.
**Expert insight:** ISO 45001 (2018) is global standard. James Reason's *Human Error* (1990) is foundational. Bird's Triangle (1 serious : 10 minor : 30 damage : 600 near-miss) underlines near-miss reporting value.
**Takeaways:**
- Near-miss reporting is highest-value safety signal.
- SMS makes safety systemic, not heroic.
- Investigate to learn, not blame.

## 251. OSHA Recordkeeping (300, 301, 300A)
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US OSHA-mandated injury/illness recordkeeping: Form 300 (log of cases); Form 301 (incident report); Form 300A (annual summary, posted Feb-April). Required for most employers with 11+ employees.
**Application:** Recordable cases: medical treatment beyond first aid; days away from work; restricted duty; transfer to another job; loss of consciousness. Annual electronic submission for some industries (E-1904). Underreporting penalties significant.
**Case Study:** A US manufacturer received OSHA inspection after an injury fatality. Records review found 38 unrecorded injuries over 18 months — classified as "first aid only" but actually meeting recordable criteria. Citation: $312K (willful per case). Internal review: managers had been pressured to under-report to lower incident rates. Remediation: HR reclaimed recordkeeping from operations; trained safety team; recordable rates tripled (now accurate); but actual injury counts declined as root causes addressed.
**Expert insight:** OSHA: 30%+ of inspections find recordkeeping violations. National Council on Compensation Insurance (NCCI) ties records to insurance rates — incentive to under-report.
**Takeaways:**
- Recordable criteria are technical — train recorders.
- Under-reporting masks problems and triggers OSHA action.
- HR ownership of recordkeeping reduces operational bias.

## 252. Workers' Compensation & Return-to-Work
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Insurance/benefit system providing medical care and wage replacement for work-related injury/illness. State-administered in US; varies by state. UK uses employer's liability insurance + statutory sick pay system.
**Application:** Process: report injury → medical treatment → claim filing → adjudication → benefits. Return-to-work (RTW): modified duty; gradual return; ergonomic adjustments; case management. Effective RTW reduces total claim cost 40%+.
**Case Study:** A US logistics firm averaged 87 days lost per claim. Introduced structured RTW programme: medical case manager assignment within 48 hours; modified duty options across departments; physical therapy on-site; manager training on RTW conversations. Within 24 months: average lost days dropped to 32; claim costs reduced 41%; injured employees reported higher engagement (vs prior stigma). Insurance premium reduced.
**Expert insight:** NCCI: structured RTW saves 25-50% per claim. Modified duty within 7 days reduces total claim cost 60%+. Disability Management Employers Coalition (DMEC) tracks practices.
**Takeaways:**
- RTW within 7 days is the high-value window.
- Modified duty options need pre-defined catalogue.
- Case management beats passive claim handling.

## 253. Drug-Free Workplace / DOT Drug Testing
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US Drug-Free Workplace Act (1988): federal contractors must maintain DFW programmes. DOT regulations (49 CFR Part 40): mandatory drug/alcohol testing for safety-sensitive transportation workers (CDL drivers, pilots, etc.).
**Application:** Testing types: pre-employment; random; post-accident; reasonable suspicion; return-to-duty; follow-up. Substances: 5-panel (THC, cocaine, opiates, PCP, amphetamines); DOT expanded to include synthetic opioids (2023).
**Case Study:** A trucking firm employed 1,800 CDL drivers. Audit revealed: random testing rate at 31% (vs DOT-required 50% annually); failed test follow-ups inconsistent; reasonable suspicion training inadequate. FMCSA audit: $186K civil penalty + monitored compliance. Remediation: third-party administrator (TPA) engaged; quarterly random testing draws; manager training; substance abuse professional (SAP) referrals. Compliance restored within 6 months.
**Expert insight:** FMCSA Clearinghouse (2020) tracks positive tests/refusals across employers. SAMHSA-certified labs only. UK testing limited to safety-sensitive roles; voluntary in most.
**Takeaways:**
- Compliance is technical — outsource to specialist TPA.
- Random testing draws must be genuinely random.
- Marijuana state legalisation complicates federal testing.

## 254. Medical Marijuana State Implications
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** US tension: marijuana legal under many state laws (38 medical, 24 recreational) but illegal under federal Controlled Substances Act. Employer policies must navigate ADA accommodations, state employment protections, federal contractor requirements.
**Application:** Many states (NY, NJ, CT, IL) prohibit adverse employment action based solely on off-duty marijuana use or medical marijuana cardholder status. Safety-sensitive roles still allow termination. Federal contractors must maintain federal DFW compliance regardless of state law.
**Case Study:** A NJ manufacturer fired a warehouse worker after positive THC test. Worker held NJ medical marijuana card; ADA accommodation request submitted. State court: termination violated NJ CREAMM Act; reinstatement + $42K back pay. Employer revised policy: testing for safety-sensitive roles only; case-by-case accommodation for medical card holders; impairment-based discipline (not test-based) for non-safety roles.
**Expert insight:** NCSL tracks state marijuana laws. Federal preemption mostly absent for employment. SHRM guidance updated regularly. Multistate employers face complex policy patchwork.
**Takeaways:**
- State law variation requires location-specific policies.
- Safety-sensitive roles maintain testing latitude.
- Impairment-based discipline more defensible than test-based.

## 255. Business Continuity & Disaster Recovery
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Business Continuity Planning (BCP) = strategy ensuring critical functions continue during/after disruption. Disaster Recovery (DR) = restoring IT systems and operations. HR's role: people aspects (communication, payroll, return, support).
**Application:** HR BCP elements: emergency communication channels; remote work readiness; payroll continuity; succession for key roles; mental health support; physical safety. Templates: ISO 22301; FEMA frameworks.
**Case Study:** A UK insurance firm tested its BCP via 2020 pandemic. Pre-pandemic plan focused on physical site loss; not designed for distributed remote work at scale. Within 72 hours: 4,200 staff working remotely; payroll continued; communications maintained via cascade. Issues surfaced: wellbeing support inadequate; new manager-team protocols needed; technology gaps for frontline ops. Plan rewrite (2021): added "people-centric" disruption scenarios; mental health support protocols; technology resilience standards. Subsequent stress tests (2023 cyber incident) demonstrated capability.
**Expert insight:** ISO 22301 is global BCP standard. BCI (Business Continuity Institute) certifies professionals. Most BCP plans pre-2020 underestimated people-centric disruptions.
**Takeaways:**
- People-centric BCP scenarios essential.
- Test BCP annually — discovery during real crisis is too late.
- HR's BCP role broader than imagined pre-2020.

## 258. Enterprise Risk Management — ERM / HR's Role
**List:** A
**Definition:** ERM = enterprise-wide approach to identifying, assessing, managing, and monitoring risk. HR's role: people-related risks (talent, succession, conduct, culture, regulatory, wellbeing).
**Application:** HR risks in ERM register: key person loss; succession gaps; harassment/discrimination claims; culture risk; data breach; regulatory non-compliance; wellbeing/safety. Risk ratings: probability × impact. Mitigation strategies; ownership; monitoring.

## 259. Employment Practices Liability — EPL
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Insurance coverage for employment-related claims: wrongful termination, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, wage/hour, breach of contract. Critical risk-management tool for HR.
**Application:** EPL policies: typically $1-25M coverage; deductibles $25K-$250K+. Coverage: defence + indemnity. Claims-made basis (not occurrence). Vendor management: brokers, panel counsel. Risk-engineering services often included.
**Case Study:** A US mid-size firm faced 12 EEOC charges over 24 months — disparate impact in hiring, retaliation claims, wage-hour issues. Without EPL, defence costs alone would have exceeded $2.4M (averaging $200K per claim). With $5M EPL policy ($150K deductible): paid deductible × 12 = $1.8M (much lower than uninsured exposure); panel counsel managed defence; settlements averaged $80K each. Lesson: EPL is mandatory for risk-prudent firms.
**Expert insight:** Marsh, Aon, AIG, Beazley lead EPL insurance market. Premium escalation post-#MeToo (2018+). Workplace harassment claims drive 30%+ of claims.
**Takeaways:**
- EPL is cost-effective for firms 100+ employees.
- Policy terms vary widely — review with broker.
- Risk engineering services often included free.

## 260. Data Breach Response Planning
**List:** B (Case Study)
**Definition:** Pre-planned response to data breach involving employee/applicant data: containment, investigation, regulatory notification (72-hour GDPR), affected-party notification, remediation, communication.
**Application:** Response plan: incident response team; legal counsel engaged immediately; forensics; regulatory notification (ICO for UK; HHS for HIPAA in US; state AGs for state breach laws); affected employee notification with ID protection; credit monitoring offer; post-incident review.
**Case Study:** A UK retailer suffered a payroll vendor breach exposing 12,000 employee records (NI numbers, bank details). ICO notified within 60 hours (within 72-hour GDPR deadline). Affected employees notified within 7 days; credit monitoring offered. ICO investigation: no penalty (good response). Remediation: vendor security audit; contract strengthened; quarterly cyber drills. Lesson: prompt regulator notification + transparent communication mitigates penalties.
**Expert insight:** GDPR fines reaching 4% of global revenue. IBM Cost of a Data Breach: average $4.5M per breach (2023). 76% involve human factor (HR data is high-value target).
**Takeaways:**
- 72-hour GDPR notification clock is strict.
- Affected-employee communication should precede media disclosure.
- Vendor due diligence prevents most breaches.

## 261. Privacy & Monitoring — ADA, GINA, ECPA
**List:** A
**Definition:** US privacy laws affecting HR: ADA (medical info confidentiality, separate files); GINA (genetic info bar); ECPA (Electronic Communications Privacy Act — limits monitoring of communications). UK: GDPR + DPA 2018 cover similar ground.
**Application:** Practical implications: medical/disability records separate from personnel; no family medical history collection; consent for workplace monitoring (or transparent policy under ECPA exceptions); limit data collection to necessary; right of access for employees.
**Expert insight:** EEOC enforces ADA/GINA. FTC and state AGs enforce broader privacy. UK ICO enforces GDPR/DPA. McKinsey: 64% of organisations expanded monitoring during COVID — many created privacy compliance issues.
**Takeaways:**
- Medical info must be in separate confidential file.
- Monitoring requires transparent policy + sometimes consent.
- Privacy law landscape fragmenting — multi-state US exposure.
